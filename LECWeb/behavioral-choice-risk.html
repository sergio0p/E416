Behavioral Choice Under Risk

Lotteries

Assume we have $n$ states of the world.

Definition: A lottery is a list of prizes and probabilities, $$\ell=\bigl((x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n),(p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_n)\bigr),$$ where $x_k \in \R^m$ is the prize the lottery gives when state $k$ occurs, and $p_k\ge 0$ is the probability that state $k$ occurs.

The Certain Lottery

The lottery that gives prize $x$ with probability one (with certainty) is denoted by: $$\delta_x=\bigl((x),(1)\bigr).$$

[TikZ Diagram — source lines 570-574] Tree with root $\delta_x$ and a single branch labeled $1$ leading to node $x$.

Expectation and Variance

The expected value of a lottery $\ell_1=\bigl((x_1,\ldots,x_n),(p_1,\ldots,p_n)\bigr)$ is: $$\E[\ell_1]=p_1\cdot x_1+p_2\cdot x_2 +\ldots+ p_n\cdot x_n = \sum_{i=1}^n p_i\cdot x_i;$$ and the variance of a lottery $\ell$ is: $$\Var[\ell_1]=\sum_{i=1}^n p_i\cdot (x_i-\E[\ell_1])^2.$$

Composition of Lotteries

Given two lotteries, $\ell_1=\bigl((x_1,\ldots,x_n),(p_1,\ldots,p_n)\bigr)$ and $\ell_2=\bigl((y_1,\ldots,y_m),(q_1,\ldots,q_m)\bigr)$ and a number $\alpha$ in the interval $(0,1)$, we can create a compound lottery $\ell$ that plays $\ell_1$ with probability $\alpha$ and $\ell_2$ with probability $1-\alpha$.

[TikZ Diagram — source lines 587-603] Two-stage tree: root $\ell$ branches to $\ell_1$ (prob $\alpha$) and $\ell_2$ (prob $1-\alpha$); $\ell_1$ branches to $x_1,x_2$ with probs $p, 1-p$; $\ell_2$ branches to $y_1,y_2$ with probs $q, 1-q$. Reduced form: $$\ell=\alpha\ell_1\oplus (1-\alpha)\ell_2 = \bigl((x_1,x_2,y_1,y_2),(\alpha p,\alpha(1-p),(1-\alpha)q,(1-\alpha)(1-q))\bigr).$$

Preferences Over Lotteries

Given two lotteries $\ell_a$ and $\ell_b$ such that a decision maker (DM) chooses $\ell_a$ over $\ell_b$, the following statements are equivalent:

  • The DM judges $\ell_a$ no worse than $\ell_b$ (everyday language);
  • The DM prefers $\ell_a$ to $\ell_b$ (economics language);
  • $\ell_a\succeq_{\text{DM}} \ell_b$ (mathematics language).

For simplicity we write:

  • $\ell_a\succ \ell_b$ when $\ell_a\succeq\ell_b$ but $\ell_b\not\succeq\ell_a$ (strict preference)
  • $\ell_a\sim \ell_b$ when $\ell_a\succeq\ell_b$ and $\ell_b\succeq\ell_a$ (indifference).

Preferences Over Lotteries

  • A preference of the DM, $\succeq_{\text{DM}}$, over the set of lotteries is just the DM's ranking of lotteries.
  • We wish (for convenience) a numerical score that reflects the DM's ranking.

von Neumann & Morgenstern's Assumptions

Completeness
For any two lotteries $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$, $\ell_1\succeq \ell_2$ and/or $\ell_2\succeq \ell_1$.
Transitivity
For any lotteries $\ell_1$, $\ell_2$ and $\ell_3$, if $\ell_1\succeq \ell_2$ and $\ell_2\succeq \ell_3$ then $\ell_1\succeq \ell_3$.
Continuity
If $\ell_1\succeq\ell_2\succeq \ell_3$ then there exists $p\in[0,1]$ such that $\ell_2\sim p\ell_1\oplus(1-p)\ell_3$.
Independence
If $\ell_1\succ\ell_2$ then for any $\ell_3$ and any $0\lt p\lt 1$, $p\ell_1\oplus (1-p)\ell_3\succ p\ell_2\oplus(1-p)\ell_3$.
Expected Utility Representation
If $\succeq$ satisfies all of the above, there exists $u:\R\rightarrow\R$ such that $$\bigl((x_1,\ldots,x_n),(p_1,\ldots,p_n)\bigr)\succ \bigl((y_1,\ldots,y_m),(q_1,\ldots,q_m)\bigr)$$ if and only if $$\sum_{k=1}^n u(x_k)\cdot p_k \gt \sum_{k=1}^m u(y_k)\cdot q_k.$$

Expected Utility

We write:

$$ U(\ell_1)=u(x_1)\cdot p_1+\ldots + u(x_n)\cdot p_n, $$

and refer to $U$ as the expected utility and to $u$ as the utility for money.

How to Use Expected Utility

  1. List the states of the world: $\omega_1$, $\omega_2$, …, $\omega_n$.
  2. List states' probabilities: $p_1$, $p_2$, …, $p_n$.
  3. For each possible action:
    • List its possible outcomes (prizes): $x_1$, $x_2$, …, $x_n$.
    • Obtain outcomes' utilities: $u(x_1)$, $u(x_2)$, …, $u(x_n)$.
    • Calculate the expectation of the utility: $$p_1\cdot u(x_1)+p_2\cdot u(x_2)+\ldots+p_n\cdot u(x_n).$$
  4. Choose the action that delivers the highest expected utility.

Risk Aversion

Let's go back to expected utility theory; consider the two lotteries: $$\ell_1=\bigl((100,102),(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\bigr) \quad\text{and}\quad \delta_{101}=\bigl((101),(1)\bigr).$$

We have $$ U(\ell_1)= u(100)\cdot \tfrac12 + u(102)\cdot \tfrac12 \quad\text{and}\quad U(\delta_{101})=u(101)\cdot 1.$$

Risk Aversion

$$U(\ell_1)= u(100)\cdot \tfrac12 + u(102)\cdot \tfrac12 \quad\text{and}\quad U(\delta_{101})=u(101).$$

$$U(\ell_1)-U(\delta_{101})=\Bigl[ u(102)-u(101)-\bigl( u(101)-u(100) \bigr) \Bigr] \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}$$

Where $u(102)-u(101)\simeq \mathrm{M}u(101)$ and $u(101)-u(100)\simeq \mathrm{M}u(100)$.

$U(\ell_1)\gt U(\delta_{101}) \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{M}u(101)\gt \mathrm{M}u(100)$.

$U(\ell_1)=U(\delta_{101}) \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{M}u(101)=\mathrm{M}u(100)$.

$U(\ell_1)\lt U(\delta_{101}) \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{M}u(101)\lt \mathrm{M}u(100)$.

Expected Utility Theory

Attitudes Towards Risk

  1. Diminishing marginal utility, $u$ is concave, $u^{\prime\prime}\lt 0\Rightarrow$ the consumer is risk-averse. $$U(X)\lt u(\E[X]) \quad\text{for all } X$$
  2. Increasing marginal utility, $u$ is convex, $u^{\prime\prime}\gt 0\Rightarrow$ the consumer is risk-loving. $$U(X)\gt u(\E[X]) \quad\text{for all } X$$
  3. Constant marginal utility, $u$ is affine, $u^{\prime\prime}=0\Rightarrow$ the consumer is risk-neutral. $$U(X)=u(\E[X]) \quad\text{for all } X$$

Measuring the Degree of Risk-Aversion

The Arrow-Pratt or Absolute Measure

Definition
The Arrow-Pratt absolute measure of risk-aversion of an agent with vN-M utility $u$ at wealth level $w$ is: $$\rho_{u}(w)=\dfrac{-u^{\prime\prime}(w)}{u^{\prime}(w)}.$$

If for two individuals with vN-M utilities $u$ and $\widetilde{u}$ we have that $\rho_{u}(w)\gt \rho_{\widetilde{u}}(w)$ for all wealth levels $w$, then we say that the agent with utility $u$ is more risk-averse than the agent with utility $\widetilde{u}$.

Prospect Theory

Prospect theory is a behavioral alternative to Expected utility theory.

Notice that “prospect” has the same meaning as “lottery”. Under prospect theory, the utility of a prospect $\ell=(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{p})$ is: $$ U^P(\ell)=\pi(p_1)\cdot \nu(x_1)+\ldots+\pi(p_n)\nu(x_n),$$ where:

  • $\nu(x)$ is the utility of obtaining the prize $x$ with certainty;
  • $\pi$ is a function that “distorts” probabilities in a special way.

Beliefs

In the standard model, individuals update their (probabilities) beliefs using:

Bayes' Rule
Given two events $A$ and $B$ such that $B$ is possible, $\Pr(B)\gt 0$, then the probability of $A$ given that $B$ occurred is: $$\Pr(A\mid B)=\dfrac{\Pr(A\cap B)}{\Pr(B)}.$$

Later, we shall see how beliefs (prior probabilities) are “formed” in the standard model…

Bayesian Updating

Let's consider two alternative hypotheses regarding some coin:

(U)
The coin is unbiased, $\Pr[H\mid U]=\Pr[T\mid U]=1/2$.
(B)
The coin is biased towards heads, $\Pr[H\mid B]=3/4$ and $\Pr[T\mid B]=1/4$.

Ann and Bart believe that only these two hypotheses are possible but they ascribe different likelihoods. Ann's prior probabilities are $\Pr^A[U]=\Pr^A[B]=1/2$ and Bart's are $\Pr^B[U]=1/3$ and $\Pr^B[B]=2/3$.

Bayesian Updating

Let $p_t(h^t)$ be Ann's belief that the hypothesis $U$ is true given that the particular history of coin tosses, $h^t$, was observed. We have that: $$p_0(\emptyset)=1/2,$$ $$p_1(T)=\Pr^A[U\mid T]=\dfrac{\Pr^A[U\cap T]}{\Pr^A[T]}=\dfrac{p_0\cdot \tfrac12}{p_0\cdot \tfrac12 +(1-p_0)\cdot \tfrac14},$$ $$p_t(h^{t-1},T)=\dfrac{p_{t-1}(h^{t-1})\cdot \tfrac12}{p_{t-1}(h^{t-1})\cdot \tfrac12 +(1-p_{t-1}(h^{t-1}))\cdot \tfrac14}.$$

In sum: given some coin toss outcome $H$ or $T$ and knowing the prior probability of the hypothesis $U$ being true, we are able to compute the posterior (updated) probability that the hypothesis $U$ is true.

Result: If two individuals assign positive probabilities to the same set of hypotheses, it does not matter if their priors differ. In the limit, as more information becomes available, their posteriors will merge. They will think alike.

Prospect Theory

Under Prospect theory, $$ U^P(\ell)=\pi(p_1)\cdot \nu(x_1)+\ldots+\pi(p_n)\nu(x_n),$$ we assume the following:

  • $\nu$ is continuous, increasing, and for some reference prize ($r$), $\nu^{\prime\prime}(x)\gt 0$ for $x\lt r$ (risk-loving behavior for losses) and $\nu^{\prime\prime}(x)\lt 0$ for $x\gt r$ (risk-averse behavior for gains).
  • For small $p$, $\pi(p)\gt p$ and $\pi(p)+\pi(1-p)\le 1$. That is, $\pi$ overestimates the probability of unlikely events and underestimates the probability of likely events.

Prospect Theory

Under Prospect theory, $$ U^P(\ell)=\pi(p_1)\cdot \nu(x_1)+\ldots+\pi(p_n)\nu(x_n),$$ in particular, the following functions are commonly used. They satisfy all the assumptions in the previous slide. $$\nu(x)=\begin{cases}(x-r)^\alpha&\text{if } x\ge r,\\ -\lambda\cdot (r-x)^\beta&\text{if } x\le r.\end{cases}$$ $$\pi(p)=\dfrac{p^\gamma}{\bigl(p^\gamma+(1-p)^\gamma\bigr)^{1/\gamma}},$$ in which $0\lt \alpha,\beta,\gamma\lt 1$ and $\lambda\gt 1$. These assumptions imply loss aversion and that low probabilities are overestimated while high probabilities are underestimated.

Ambiguity Aversion

Max Min Expected Utility Theory

Max Min Expected Utility Theory is an alternative theory to Expected Utility Theory that allows ambiguity aversion.

To use max min utility theory:

  1. Consider an alternative (lottery).
  2. For each possible probability distribution: compute the expected utility of the alternative.
  3. The minimum of all expected utilities in (2) is the value of the alternative.
  4. Choose the alternative that has the highest value.

Ambiguity Aversion

Max Min Expected Utility Theory

Example:

  • A coin is either fair or both sides display “heads”.
  • There are two possible probability distributions: $p=1/2$ or $p=1$ where $p$ is the probability of “heads”.
  • Lottery #1 (L1) pays \$500 if “tails”.
  • Lottery #2 (L2) pays \$100 if “heads”.
$$ U(L1)=\min_{p\in \{ \frac12,1\} } p\,u(0)+(1-p)u(500)=u(0)$$ $$ U(L2)=\min_{p\in \{ \frac12,1\} } p\,u(100)+(1-p)u(0)=\tfrac12 u(100)+\tfrac12 u(0)$$

So lottery #2 is always better for someone who is ambiguity averse and uses the max min rule.
