Thaler’s Examples

Insurance Against Small Annoyances

“A former colleague of mine, a professor of finance, prides himself on being a thoroughly rational man. Long ago he adopted a clever strategy to deal with life’s misfortunes. At the beginning of each year he establishes a target donation to the local United Way charity. Then, if anything untoward happens to him during the year, for example an undeserved speeding ticket, he simply deducts this loss from the United Way account. He thinks of it as an insurance policy against small annoyances.”

Thaler (1999), p. 183

A Vacation in Switzerland

“A few years ago I gave a talk to a group of executives in Switzerland. After the conference my wife and I spent a week visiting the area. At that time the Swiss franc was at an all-time high relative to the US dollar, so the usual high prices in Switzerland were astronomical. Had I received the same fee a week earlier for a talk in New York though, the vacation would have been much less enjoyable.”

The King-Size Quilt

“A friend of mine was once shopping for a quilted bedspread. She went to a department store and was pleased to find a model she liked on sale. The spreads came in three sizes: double, queen and king. The usual prices for these quilts were $\$200$, $\$250$ and $\$300$ respectively, but during the sale they were all priced at only $\$150$. My friend bought the king-size quilt and was quite pleased with her purchase, though the quilt did hang a bit over the sides of her double bed.”

Thaler (1999), p. 184

The Three Components of Mental Accounting

  • Outcomes and decisions. How outcomes are perceived and experienced, and how decisions are made and later evaluated. Mental accounts supply the inputs for both the ex ante and the ex post cost–benefit analysis. In the quilt example, the value of the “deal” (transaction utility) enters the purchase decision.
  • Assigning activities to accounts. Spending is grouped into categories (housing, food, …), sometimes held to budgets; money to spend is labeled as flows (regular income versus windfalls) and as stocks (cash on hand, home equity, pension wealth). The Swiss vacation was paid out of a “Swiss lecture” account; the United Way account is a flexible way to absorb small losses.
  • How often accounts are evaluated. Accounts can be balanced daily, weekly or yearly, and defined narrowly or broadly: choice bracketing. Poker players are told to “never count your money while you’re sitting at the table,” and the same advice holds for other risky decisions, such as investing.

The Value Function

Thaler assumes the value function (i.e., the utility function $u$) from Prospect Theory:

  • The reference point is normalized to zero, and $u(0)=0$.
  • Risk loving in the loss domain: $u^{\prime}(x)$ is increasing for $x\lt 0$. Risk averse in the gain domain: $u^{\prime}(x)$ is decreasing for $x\gt 0$.
  • Loss aversion: $-u(-x)\gt u(x)$ for $x\gt 0$.

Ways of Framing Outcomes

Outcomes can be framed in three ways:

Minimal Account
Compare two options by their differences
only, ignoring everything they have in common.
Topical Account
Relate the consequences of a choice to a reference level set by the context in which the decision arises.
Comprehensive Account
Evaluate each option by its effect on total wealth: current wealth, future earnings, the outcomes of other risky holdings, and so on. Economic theory generally assumes this one.

Framing

The Minimal, Topical, Comprehensive Accounts in Action

The Jacket Example
Imagine that you are about to purchase a jacket for $\$450$ and a calculator for $\$55$. The jacket salesman informs you that the jacket you wish to buy is on sale for $\$430$ at the other branch of the store, located 20 minutes drive away. Would you make the trip to the other store?
The Calculator Example
Imagine that you are about to purchase a jacket for $\$450$ and a calculator for $\$55$. The calculator salesman informs you that the calculator you wish to buy is on sale for $\$35$ at the other branch of the store, located 20 minutes drive away. Would you make the trip to the other store?

The respective accounts in action:

  • Minimal account: $\$20$ against the opportunity cost of 20 minutes in both examples, so the answer should be the same.
  • Comprehensive account: with $W^*$ the wealth after both purchases, $u(W^*+\$20)$ against $u(W^*+\text{opportunity cost of 20 minutes})$ in both examples, so again the same answer.
  • Topical account: the saving is judged against the price of the product on sale, $u(-35)-u(-55)$ for the calculator versus $u(-430)-u(-450)$ for the jacket. Since $u^{\prime}(x)$ is increasing for $x\lt 0$, the first is larger: people drive for the calculator discount only.

Hedonic Editing

Hedonic editing:

  • Segregate gains (because of decreasing marginal utility for gains).
  • Integrate losses (because of increasing marginal utility for losses).
  • Integrate smaller losses with larger gains (to offset loss aversion).
  • Segregate small gains (silver linings) from larger losses (because the gain function is steepest at the origin, the utility of a small gain can exceed the utility of slightly reducing a large loss).
$$\nu(x,y)=\max\left(u(x)+u(y),\,u(x+y)\right)$$

Temporal Spacing

As Hedonic Editing

  • Subjects were given two financial outcomes, $x$ and $y$ (for example, a gain of $\$30$ and a loss of $\$9$), and asked who would be happier: someone who had both on the same day, or someone who had them a week or two apart.
  • Separation in time makes it easier to keep the outcomes apart: close in time, they are integrated, $u(x+y)$; separate in time, they are segregated, $u(x)+u(y)$.
  • Under hedonic editing, the preferred spacing is the one that attains $\nu(x,y)=\max\left(u(x)+u(y),\,u(x+y)\right)$: separate in time if $u(x)+u(y)\gt u(x+y)$, close in time otherwise.

Transaction Utility

A hot day at the beach. A cold beer is worth $\$5$ to you and costs $\$4$. A friend will buy it either at a fancy resort hotel, where you expect a beer to cost $\$5$, or at a run-down grocery store, where you expect it to cost $\$3$.

The Arithmetic

${u(x)=\sqrt{x}}$ for $x\ge 0$, ${u(x)=-2\sqrt{-x}}$ for $x\lt 0$.

Price felt as a loss, segregated: ${u(5)+u(-4)}={\sqrt{5}-4}\approx-1.76$. No beer.

Price netted against the beer, integrated: ${u(5-4)}=1$. This is acquisition utility.

Add transaction utility, ${u(p^*-4)}$, with $p^*$ the price you expect:

Resort, $p^*=5$: ${1+u(1)}=2$. Buy.

Grocery store, $p^*=3$: ${1+u(-1)}=-1$. Don’t buy.

Opening and Closing Accounts

You bought two stocks for $\$100$ each. Stock A is now worth $\$125$, stock B $\$84$. You need cash and must sell one of them. A gain or loss stays on paper until the stock is sold; selling closes its account.

The Arithmetic

${u(x)=\sqrt{x}}$ for $x\ge 0$, ${u(x)=-2\sqrt{-x}}$ for $x\lt 0$.

An open account stays out of $u$; only a closed one is felt.

Sell A: $u(25)=5$.

Sell B: $u(-16)=-8$.

You sell the winner, even though taxes favor selling the loser. If B must go, sell it the same day as A: ${u(25-16)}=3\gt{u(25)+u(-16)}=-3$.

Decoupling

Dinner costs $\$100$. You pay cash at the table, or you put it on the credit card and see it weeks later on a $\$676$ monthly bill, next to $\$576$ of other charges.

The Arithmetic

${u(x)=\sqrt{x}}$ for $x\ge 0$, ${u(x)=-2\sqrt{-x}}$ for $x\lt 0$.

Cash: the payment is felt on its own, $u(-100)=-20$.

Card: the payment is folded into the bill, ${u(-676)-u(-576)}={-52+48}=-4$.

The same $\$100$ hurts a fifth as much on the card.

Budgeting

A lecture in Switzerland pays a $\$2{,}500$ fee, and the week of vacation after it costs $\$900$. Either the fee and the vacation share a “Swiss lecture” account, or the fee came a week earlier, for a lecture in New York, and sits in another account.

The Arithmetic

${u(x)=\sqrt{x}}$ for $x\ge 0$, ${u(x)=-2\sqrt{-x}}$ for $x\lt 0$.

One account, integrated: ${u(2500-900)}={u(1600)}=40$.

Two accounts, segregated: ${u(2500)+u(-900)}={50-60}=-10$.

Same fee, same prices: the Swiss lecture account takes the sting out of the vacation.

Bracketing

Samuelson offers a colleague a coin flip: win $\$200$ or lose $\$100$. The colleague turns down one flip but would take a hundred of them.

The Arithmetic

${u(x)=\sqrt{x}}$ for $x\ge 0$, ${u(x)=-2\sqrt{-x}}$ for $x\lt 0$.

One flip: ${\tfrac12u(200)+\tfrac12u(-100)}\approx{7.07-10}=-2.93$. Reject.

Two flips, each judged alone: ${2\times(-2.93)}=-5.86$.

Two flips judged together: win $\$400$ or $\$100$, or lose $\$200$, with probabilities $\tfrac14$, $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac14$: ${\tfrac14u(400)+\tfrac12u(100)+\tfrac14u(-200)}\approx{5+5-7.07}=2.93$. Accept.
