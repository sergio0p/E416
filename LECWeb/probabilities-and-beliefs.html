Extracting $u$ from choices

Under Expected Utility (Standard Model)

The idea is the same as in choice under certainty. There we defined the utility of every basket on the diagonal, $u(x,x)=x$, and gave any other basket the utility of the diagonal basket it is indifferent to. Under expected utility the ruler is no longer the diagonal but the family of lotteries between the best and the worst prize.

Standard Gamble (Probability Equivalent)
Let $b$ be the best prize and $w$ the worst, and set $u(b)=1$ and $u(w)=0$. For any other prize $x$, find the probability $p$ that makes the agent indifferent between $x$ for sure and the lottery that pays $b$ with probability $p$ and $w$ with probability $1-p$: $$\delta_x\sim\left((b,w),(p,1-p)\right).$$ The expected utility of the lottery is $p\cdot u(b)+(1-p)\cdot u(w)=p$, so $u(x)=p$.

This is the von Neumann–Morgenstern construction turned into a question, and it is the standard way of measuring utility in health economics.

Example. Three prizes. Ann's choices between sure prizes reveal the ranking: San Pellegrino $\succ$ KitKat $\succ$ banana.

  1. Fix the scale: $u(\text{banana})=0$ and $u(\text{San Pellegrino})=1$.
  2. Ask Ann: a KitKat for sure, or the lottery $\left((\text{San Pellegrino},\text{banana}),(p,1-p)\right)$?
  3. She takes the KitKat at $p=1/2$, so $u(\text{KitKat})\gt 1/2$: raise $p$. She takes the lottery at $p=3/4$, so $u(\text{KitKat})\lt 3/4$: lower $p$.
  4. Keep going until she is indifferent, say at $p^*=0.7$. Then $u(\text{KitKat})=0.7$.

Extracting $p$ from choices

Under Expected Utility (Standard Model)

Same trick, new unknown. In the standard model beliefs are probabilities. Ann's probability that it rains in Chapel Hill tomorrow is not written anywhere: we extract it from her choices, one indifference at a time.

Matching Probability
Take two prizes, $b\succ w$, and an event $A$. A bet on $A$ pays $b$ if $A$ happens and $w$ if it does not. Find the probability $p$ that makes Ann indifferent between the bet on $A$ and the lottery that pays $b$ with probability $p$ and $w$ with probability $1-p$: $$(b \text{ if } A,\; w \text{ if not } A)\sim\left((b,w),(p,1-p)\right).$$ The expected utility of the bet is $\Pr[A]\cdot u(b)+(1-\Pr[A])\cdot u(w)$ and the expected utility of the lottery is $p\cdot u(b)+(1-p)\cdot u(w)$. They are equal only if $\Pr[A]=p$.

Notice that $u$ cancels: all we used is $u(b)\gt u(w)$. So we do not need to know Ann's utility to extract her beliefs. Any two prizes she can rank will do.

Example. Prizes as before, San Pellegrino $\succ$ banana, and $A$ = rain in Chapel Hill tomorrow.

  1. Ask Ann: a San Pellegrino if it rains tomorrow and a banana if not, or the lottery $\left((\text{San Pellegrino},\text{banana}),(p,1-p)\right)$?
  2. She takes the bet at $p=1/2$, so $\Pr^A[\text{rain}]\gt 1/2$: raise $p$. She takes the lottery at $p=3/4$, so $\Pr^A[\text{rain}]\lt 3/4$: lower $p$.
  3. Keep going until she is indifferent, say at $p^*=0.6$. Then $\Pr^A[\text{rain}]=0.6$.

Beliefs

In the standard model, individuals update their (probabilities) beliefs using:

Bayes' Rule
Given two events $A$ and $B$ such that $B$ is possible, $\Pr(B)\gt 0$, then the probability of $A$ given that $B$ occurred is: $$\Pr(A\mid B)=\dfrac{\Pr(A\cap B)}{\Pr(B)}.$$

Later, we shall see how beliefs (prior probabilities) are “formed” in the standard model…

Bayesian Updating

Let's consider two alternative hypotheses regarding some coin:

(U)
The coin is unbiased, $\Pr[H\mid U]=\Pr[T\mid U]=1/2$.
(B)
The coin is biased towards heads, $\Pr[H\mid B]=3/4$ and $\Pr[T\mid B]=1/4$.

Ann and Bart believe that only these two hypotheses are possible but they ascribe different likelihoods. Ann's prior probabilities are $\Pr^A[U]=\Pr^A[B]=1/2$ and Bart's are $\Pr^B[U]=1/3$ and $\Pr^B[B]=2/3$.

Bayesian Updating

Let $p_t(h^t)$ be Ann's belief that the hypothesis $U$ is true given that the particular history of coin tosses, $h^t$, was observed. We have that: $$p_0(\emptyset)=1/2,$$ $$p_1(T)=\Pr^A[U\mid T]=\dfrac{\Pr^A[U\cap T]}{\Pr^A[T]}=\dfrac{p_0\cdot \tfrac12}{p_0\cdot \tfrac12 +(1-p_0)\cdot \tfrac14},$$ $$p_t(h^{t-1},T)=\dfrac{p_{t-1}(h^{t-1})\cdot \tfrac12}{p_{t-1}(h^{t-1})\cdot \tfrac12 +(1-p_{t-1}(h^{t-1}))\cdot \tfrac14}.$$

In sum: given some coin toss outcome $H$ or $T$ and knowing the prior probability of the hypothesis $U$ being true, we are able to compute the posterior (updated) probability that the hypothesis $U$ is true.

Result: If two individuals assign positive probabilities to the same set of hypotheses, it does not matter if their priors differ. In the limit, as more information becomes available, their posteriors will merge. They will think alike.

Heuristics

Heuristics are rules of thumb that deliver satisfactory outcomes without requiring high computational costs.

Probability Estimation

Common heuristics used to evaluate probabilities:

  1. Availability
  2. Representativeness
  3. Base rate neglect
  4. ‘Law’ of small numbers (gambler's fallacy and ‘hot hand’)
1. Availability
We judge how likely an event is by how easily examples of it come to mind.
Example

After a plane crash makes the news, people overestimate the risk of flying: a crash is vivid and easy to recall, the car accidents of the same week are not.

Are there more English words that start with K, or that have K as the third letter? Most people say “start with K”, because those are easier to think of. There are about twice as many with K in third place.

2. Representativeness
We judge how likely something is by how much it resembles our stereotype of it, and ignore how common it is.
Example

Linda is 31, single, outspoken, and was a philosophy major deeply concerned with social justice. Which is more likely?

(a) Linda is a bank teller.
(b) Linda is a bank teller and active in the feminist movement.

Most people pick (b). But (b) is a special case of (a): every Linda in (b) is also in (a), so (b) cannot be more likely.

3. Base rate neglect
We go by the evidence alone and forget how common the event was to begin with.
Example

In a city, 85% of the cabs are Green and 15% are Blue. A cab was involved in a hit-and-run at night. A witness says it was Blue. Tested under the same conditions, the witness identifies the color correctly 80% of the time. How likely is it that the cab was Blue?

Most people answer about $0.8$. Bayes' rule gives $$\begin{aligned}&\Pr[\text{Blue}\mid\text{says Blue}]\\ &=\dfrac{0.15\cdot 0.8}{0.15\cdot 0.8+0.85\cdot 0.2}=\dfrac{0.12}{0.29}\approx 0.41.\end{aligned}$$ The base rate, $15\%$ Blue, was ignored.

4. ‘Law’ of small numbers
We expect a small sample to look like the whole population.
Example

Gambler's fallacy. After five reds in a row at roulette, black feels “due”. The wheel has no memory: black is still $18/38$.

Hot hand. A basketball player who hit three shots in a row is “hot”, so the next shot feels more likely to go in than her season average.

Both come from the same belief: a short run must balance out (fallacy) or must continue (hot hand), when a short run is just what randomness looks like.

Biases

  1. Overconfidence
    1. Overestimation
    2. Overplacement
    3. Overprecision
  2. Underconfidence
  3. Self-serving bias
    1. Pavlovian withdrawal
    2. Confirmatory bias
    3. Self-attribution bias
    4. Visceral fit
  4. Projection bias
  5. Magical thoughts
    1. Tempting fate
    2. Contagion
Overconfidence
Being more sure of yourself, or of your judgment, than the facts justify. It comes in three kinds.
Example

Ask a class to predict their exam score, to rank themselves in the class, and to give a range they are 90% sure contains the class average. Most predict too high, most rank themselves above the median, and most ranges miss.

Overestimation
Thinking you will do better, faster, or more than you actually will.
Example

The planning fallacy. Professor Parreiras predicts a quick walk from Gardner to Fetzer Hall and arrives late to class.

Overplacement
Thinking you are better than other people.
Example

The better-than-average effect: most drivers rate themselves above the median driver, which cannot be true of most drivers.

Overprecision
Being too sure that your estimate is right.
Example

Ask people for a range they are 90% sure contains the length of the Nile. Fewer than half of the ranges contain it.

Underconfidence
Thinking you will do worse, or rank lower, than you actually do.
Example

The hard-easy effect: on hard tasks people overestimate their score but rank themselves too low; on easy tasks the reverse. Ask who is below average at juggling and most hands go up, which cannot be true either.

Self-serving bias
We judge what is true, or what is fair, in the direction that benefits us.
Example

Two students read the same file about a car accident, one as the injured party and one as the driver, and are asked what a fair settlement would be. Each expects the judge to side with them. The further apart their answers, the more often they fail to settle, and both lose.

Pavlovian withdrawal
When a train of thought heads toward a bad outcome, we flinch and drop it before it is finished. The futures we do think through are the rosy ones, so our beliefs come out too optimistic.
Example

Depressive realism. Students press a button and a light sometimes comes on; afterwards they rate how much control they had over it. Non-depressed students overrate their control. Depressed students, whose flinch is weaker, get it right: sadder but wiser.

Confirmatory bias
We look for evidence, and read it, in a way that supports what we already believe.
Example

Supporters and opponents of the death penalty read the same two studies, one finding that it deters crime and one finding that it does not. Each side rates the study on its side as the better one, and both leave more convinced than they came.

Self-attribution bias
Success is my skill; failure is bad luck.
Example

A good grade is deserved, a bad one is an unfair exam. Investors credit their skill for gains and blame the market for losses, so after a run of gains they trade more, and their returns fall.

Visceral fit
What you feel in your body right now makes the states of the world that match it seem more likely.
Example

People asked on a hot day believe more in global warming than people asked on a cold day. Thirsty people rate a drought as more likely.

Projection bias
We expect our future tastes to be what they are today.
Example

Cold-weather gear ordered on an unusually cold day is returned more often once the weather turns. Shopping when hungry fills the cart with more than you will eat.

Magical thoughts
Believing in causal links that the physical world does not allow.
Example

A lucky shirt on game day, knocking on wood, not saying the name of the disease.

Tempting fate
Believing that a careless or boastful act invites the bad outcome.
Example

Students who skipped the reading feel more likely to be called on in class. Saying “I have never had a flat tire” feels like asking for one.

Contagion
An object keeps the essence of whatever touched it.
Example

People refuse a glass of juice a sterilized cockroach was dipped in, and will not wear a freshly laundered sweater that once belonged to a murderer.
