Reference-Dependent Preferences

Individuals use as reference points:

  1. Other's choices/consumption
  2. Past “utility”
  3. Anticipated “utility”
    (i.e., what they expect, not expected utility theory)

Menu Effects

Menu Effect
What an individual chooses depends on what else is on the menu. Adding or removing an option that nobody ends up choosing can still change which of the remaining options is picked.

Standard theory rules this out: if $\mathbf{x}$ is chosen when $\mathbf{y}$ is available, then adding a third option $\mathbf{z}$ should never make $\mathbf{y}$ the choice. The menu is supposed to be a container, not an ingredient.

Decoy Effect

Decoy Effect
A menu effect produced by adding an option that is clearly worse than one option but not clearly worse than the other. The added option—the decoy—is hardly ever chosen. Its job is to make the option that beats it look like the better deal.
Economist.com offers Price
Option 1Web subscription$59
Option 2Print subscription$125
Option 3Print + web subscription$125

Option 2 is the decoy: it costs the same as Option 3 and gives strictly less, so nobody should take it. Yet its presence makes Option 3 look like a bargain and pulls choices away from Option 1.

A Reference-Dependent Preferences Model

This model is a simplified version of Köszegi & Rabin (2006).

  • There are two goods: $X$ and $Y$.
  • There is a reference basket: $r=(\underline x,\underline y)$.
  • $u(x)$ consumption utility of good $X$.
  • $u(y)$ consumption utility of good $Y$.
  • $\mu(\Delta u)$ is the gain-loss utility function.
  • The consumer overall utility for the basket $(x,y)$ is:
$$U(x,y\mid \underline x,\underline y)= u(x)+u(y)+\mu(u(x)-u(\underline x))+\mu(u(y)-u(\underline y)).$$

cont.

  • $\mu(u(x)-u(\underline x))$ is the gain or loss in utility when the consumer deviates from the $X$-reference consumption level.
  • $\mu(u(y)-u(\underline y))$ is the gain or loss in utility when the consumer deviates from $Y$-reference consumption level.
  • The gain-loss utility function is given by: $$\mu(\Delta u)\equiv L\cdot \min(\Delta u, 0)+G\cdot \max(\Delta u, 0).$$
  • $\mu(\Delta u)$ is the gain-loss utility function.
  • $L$ is the slope of the gain-loss utility for utility losses.
  • $G$ is the slope of the gain-loss utility for utility gains.

Loss Aversion

Let $u$ be either a utility for wealth or a gain-loss utility function. The point zero is either the initial wealth or the reference point, and we assume $u(0)=0$.

Definition
We say that $u$ displays loss aversion if and only if for any point $x\gt 0$, we have that $u(x)\lt -u(-x)$.
“What should I gain by the exaction of the forfeiture? A pound of man’s flesh, taken from a man, is not so estimable, profitable neither, as flesh of muttons, beefs, or goats.” — The Merchant of Venice, Act 1, Scene 3 (proposing the bond, weighing what a forfeiture would be worth)
“Thou stick’st a dagger in me: I shall never see my gold again. Fourscore ducats at a sitting! Fourscore ducats!” — The Merchant of Venice, Act 3, Scene 1 (with Tubal, on the ducats his daughter took)

The Loss-Gain Utility

The loss-gain utility is a component of the overall utility. Its utility/disutility depends on how the consumption-utility of a good differs from the consumption-utility of its reference level.

A loss-gain utility $\mu$ as defined above displays loss aversion if and only if $L\gt G\gt 0$.

Intermediary Micro Review

Finding the optimal basket

A consumer looks for the optimal basket $(x,y)$ on the budget line $p_X\,x+p_Y\,y=I$.

  • $\tfrac{1}{p_X}$ is the amount of good $X$ one dollar buys.
  • Spending that dollar on $X$ raises utility by about $U_X\cdot\tfrac{1}{p_X}$—the marginal utility per dollar of $X$.
  • Likewise, one dollar spent on $Y$ raises utility by about $U_Y\cdot\tfrac{1}{p_Y}$.
  • If $U_X\cdot\tfrac{1}{p_X}\gt U_Y\cdot\tfrac{1}{p_Y}$, the consumer should take one dollar away from good $Y$ and spend it on good $X$: utility rises.
  • If $U_X\cdot\tfrac{1}{p_X}\lt U_Y\cdot\tfrac{1}{p_Y}$, shift one dollar the other way, from $X$ to $Y$.
  • The consumer keeps shifting dollars until no move raises utility.

That happens in one of two ways:

  • Smooth interior optimum—the marginal utilities per dollar are equal: $$\frac{U_X}{p_X}=\frac{U_Y}{p_Y}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathrm{MRS}=\frac{U_X}{U_Y}=\frac{p_X}{p_Y}.$$
  • At a kink—where the optimal basket has one good at its reference level ($x=\bar x$ or $y=\bar y$), so the indifference curve bends—the $\mathrm{MRS}$ is not a single number: it takes a one-sided value on each side. The consumer stops as soon as the price ratio lies between them, $$\mathrm{MRS}^{-}\;\ge\;\frac{p_X}{p_Y}\;\ge\;\mathrm{MRS}^{+},$$ because from a kink, shifting a dollar either way lowers the marginal utility gained. Here $\mathrm{MRS}^{+}$ is the slope along the indifference curve when the consumer increases the quantity of $X$ and decreases the quantity of $Y$, while $\mathrm{MRS}^{-}$ is the slope when the consumer decreases $X$ and increases $Y$.

A Numerical Example

Consider a traditional ($T$) consumer with utility $u_T(x,y)=\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}$, a consumer ($R$) with reference dependent utility, $$u_R(x,y\mid 20,80)=\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}+\mu(\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{20})+\mu(\sqrt{y}-\sqrt{80}),$$ where:

  • $\mu(\Delta u) = \min (\Delta u,0)+\tfrac12\cdot \max (\Delta u,0)$.
  • $(20,80)$ is the reference basket.

We assume that both consumers' incomes are $I_T=I_R=120$ and prices initially are $p_X=2$ and $p_Y=1$.

At these prices, the optimal choice for these consumers is to choose the basket $(20,80)$, which (by chance) coincides with the reference basket of the $R$ consumer.

Now suppose the price of $X$ rises to $p_X=3$ (with $p_Y=1$ and $I=120$ unchanged), so the budget line pivots about its $Y$-intercept $(0,120)$.

  • The traditional consumer moves to $(10,90)$—substituting toward the now relatively cheaper good $Y$.
  • The reference-dependent consumer moves to $(\tfrac{40}{3},80)\approx(13.3,80)$: it keeps $Y$ at its reference level $80$—dropping below the reference would hurt—so the whole adjustment falls on $X$.
