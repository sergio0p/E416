Midterm 2 FALL 25—Essay Answers

Question 2

1) Describe the minimal account. Provide an example of a decision using it. 2) Describe the topical account. Provide an example of a decision using it.

Answer. Minimal account: compare options by their differences only. Example: $\$20$ saved against a 20-minute drive, the same call for the $\$55$ calculator and the $\$450$ jacket. Topical account: judge the consequence against a reference set by the context. Example: drive to save $\$20$ on the $\$55$ calculator, but not on the $\$450$ jacket. See Mental Accounting.

Question 3

1) Explain the concept of “silver linings” in the context of mental accounting. 2) List and explain the assumptions that Thaler makes about the utility function/value function in his article on Mental Accounting. 3) For silver linings, should we integrate the gain and the loss or should we segregate them? Why or why not?

Answer. Silver lining: a small gain paired with a larger loss, e.g., $\$25$ won the day the car needs $\$200$ in repairs. Assumptions: $u(0)=0$ at the reference point; risk averse for gains, $u^{\prime}(x)$ decreasing for $x\gt 0$; risk loving for losses, $u^{\prime}(x)$ increasing for $x\lt 0$; loss aversion, $-u(-x)\gt u(x)$ for $x\gt 0$. Segregate: $u(g)+u(-L)\gt u(g-L)$ for a small gain $g$, since $u$ is steepest at the origin and flat far into losses.

Question 1

Answer according to the article “Mental Accounting Matters”. Regarding the decision frames, briefly explain the “topical account”, making sure to explain how it differs from the “minimal” and the “comprehensive” accounts.

Answer. Topical account: relate a choice’s consequences to a reference level set by the decision’s context, e.g., a discount judged against the calculator’s price. Minimal account: compare options by their differences only. Comprehensive account: judge each option by its effect on total wealth. See Mental Accounting.

Question 2

Econ. PhD students at Duke may take courses at the UNC Economics Department at no additional charge. One of their professors tells them that it would make no sense to do so, since they would be losing the money they paid for tuition at Duke. Anna has already paid tuition at Duke, but judges that the Health Economics course at UNC fits her research interests better. Should Anna enroll in Health Economics at UNC? Why or why not? Which economic concept(s) explain your recommendation? Which decision framing is the Duke professor using: the minimal, the topical, or the comprehensive account? Why?

Answer. Yes, enroll at UNC. Duke tuition is a sunk cost: paid either way, so it should not affect the choice. The UNC course costs nothing extra and fits her research better. The professor uses a topical account: he judges the course choice against a reference set by the context, the tuition already paid at Duke.

Question 6

The set-up is identical to the previous section, but now player 2 (and only player 2) has social preferences: player 2 follows the inequity aversion model of the Lecture Notes, with envy coefficient $\alpha$ and guilt coefficient $\beta$. For $x\gt 0.5$, write down player 2’s utility when saying “yes” and when saying “no”. What value of $x$ makes player 2 indifferent between “yes” and “no”? (The exam’s utility parts say “player 1”; they mean player 2, the player who says yes or no.)

Answer. With $x\gt 0.5$, player 2 gets less than player 1, $1-x\lt x$: only envy counts (inequity aversion).

$$\begin{aligned} U_2(\text{yes})&=(1-x)-\alpha\,\bigl(x-(1-x)\bigr)=1-x-\alpha\,(2x-1),\\ U_2(\text{no})&=0-0=0,\\ U_2(\text{yes})=U_2(\text{no})&\iff 1-x=\alpha\,(2x-1)\iff x=\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha}. \end{aligned}$$

Question 7

In the ultimatum game of the previous questions, and according to the book excerpt we read regarding dopamine, what could be a biological explanation for player 2 to choose “no” when player 1 makes an unfair demand, say $x\gt 0.9$? The excerpt may not refer directly to the ultimatum game but to related games.

Answer. Punishing an unfair player activates the dopamine reward system: saying “no” feels rewarding. That reward can outweigh the small amount of money player 2 gives up. Also, an offer far below what player 2 expected lowers dopamine: bad surprise, so rejection. See Brain Biology.

Question 8

You are on a diet and must decide whether to have a slice of red-velvet cake at a party on Saturday. Eating the cake gives utility $5$, and then you must exercise for hours on Sunday, utility $0$. Skipping the cake gives utility $1$, and then you relax on Sunday, utility $8$. You discount quasi-hyperbolically with $\beta=\tfrac12$ and $\delta=\tfrac13$. From the point of view of Friday, and then of Saturday, what is the utility of eating the cake and of skipping it? Which do you prefer?

Answer. $(\beta,\delta)$-model: $D(0)=1$ and $D(t)=\beta\,\delta^t$ for $t\gt 0$.

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Friday, cake:}&\quad \tfrac12\paren{\tfrac13\cdot 5+\tfrac19\cdot 0}=\tfrac56,\\ \text{Friday, skip:}&\quad \tfrac12\paren{\tfrac13\cdot 1+\tfrac19\cdot 8}=\tfrac{11}{18}\lt\tfrac56\;\Rightarrow\;\text{cake},\\ \text{Saturday, cake:}&\quad 5+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac13\cdot 0=5,\\ \text{Saturday, skip:}&\quad 1+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac13\cdot 8=\tfrac73\lt 5\;\Rightarrow\;\text{cake}. \end{aligned}$$

Same choice on both days: no preference reversal with these numbers.

Question 10

Describe in detail the (Iowa) gambling task experiment that the Bechara et al. (2004) article refers to. Hint: we played the experiment in our classroom.

Answer. Four decks, A–D; each card wins money, some cards also lose money. Decks A and B: big wins, bigger occasional losses, net loss. Decks C and D: small wins, small losses, net gain. Healthy players drift to C and D, and their skin conductance rises before risky picks, before they can say why; patients with ventromedial prefrontal damage keep picking A and B.
