Intertemporal Choice: The Standard Model

Discounted Expected Utility Model

Define $u_t$ as the utility received in period $t$. The discounted utility of a utility flow ${\mathbf{u}=(u_0,u_1,\ldots,u_t,\ldots)}$ is: $$U(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}\delta^{t}\cdot u_t$$

The discount factor $\delta$ is an exogenous parameter that reflects the agent's time preferences: the lower $\delta$, the more impatient the agent. Alternatively, $\delta$ can be interpreted as the probability that the agent survives to the next period.

General Models of Discounting

Let $D(t)$ be a function of $t$ such that ${D(0)=1}$ and ${0\lt D(t+1)\lt D(t)}$. We call $D(\cdot)$ the discount factor function, and the corresponding discounted utility is: $$U(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}D(t)\cdot u_t$$

Notice that the standard model—also called exponential discounting—is the case in which ${D(t)=\delta^t}$ for ${0\lt\delta\lt 1}$.

Behavioral Models of Intertemporal Choice

The $(\beta,\delta)$-model (quasi-hyperbolic discounting)

In the standard model (exponential utility), the discount function is: $$D(t)=\delta^t = \exp \left(-k\cdot t\right),\quad\text{where } k=-\log(\delta).$$

The $(\beta,\delta)$-model uses a different discount function: $$D(t)=\begin{cases} 1 &\text{if } t=0,\\ \beta\cdot \delta^t &\text{if } t\gt 0.\end{cases}$$

  • For streams of utilities that yield the same utility in the present (e.g., $(0,1,0)$ and $(0,0,2)$) both models generate the same results. Why?
  • For streams of utilities that yield different utilities in the present (e.g., $(1,0,0)$ and $(0,2,0)$) their results might differ. Why?
  • The $(\beta,\delta)$-model displays present bias. Why?

Behavioral Models of Intertemporal Choice

Hyperbolic discounting

The hyperbolic discounting function is: $$D(t)=\bigl(1+\alpha\cdot t \bigr)^{-\gamma/\alpha}$$ where ${\alpha\gt 0}$ and ${\gamma\gt 0}$. Notice that as ${\alpha\searrow 0}$, the discount factor converges to ${D(t)=\exp(-\gamma\cdot t)=\delta^{t}}$, where ${\delta=\exp(-\gamma)}$. Thus, exponential discounting is a limit case of hyperbolic discounting.

Dynamic Consistency

Notice that with hyperbolic discounting or with $(\beta,\delta)$ discounting, individual choices might not be dynamically consistent. That is, as time elapses, an individual might change his/her ranking over two flows of utilities even if during the elapsed time both flows delivered the same utility.

Example
Consider $\delta=0.9$ and $\beta=0.1$ and three periods $t=0,1$ and $2$. At period zero we have that $$U^{\beta,\delta}(0,1,0)=0.09\lt U^{\beta,\delta}(0,0,2)=0.081\cdot 2= 0.162.$$ But after period zero has elapsed the preferences are reversed: $$U^{\beta,\delta}(1,0)=1\gt U^{\beta,\delta}(0,2)=0.18.$$

Dynamic Consistency

When time preferences might exhibit dynamically inconsistent behavior, we need to specify how individuals will react. There are three possible alternatives:

  1. The naive approach: the individual does not realize that he/she might change his/her preferences in the future. He/she makes choices in the present period ignoring that his/her plans might be foiled (by himself/herself) in the future.
  2. Strategic approach without commitment: the individual realizes that his/her future self might behave differently than his/her present self wishes, and so he/she will act expecting that her future self will react to her choices.
  3. Strategic approach with commitment: the individual realizes that his/her future self might behave differently than his/her present self wishes, and so he/she will remove the ability of her future self to make choices by committing to the present choices.
