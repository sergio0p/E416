Inter-temporal Choice

Intertemporal Choice: The Standard Model

Discounted Expected Utility Model

In the discounted expected utility model, the per period utility of an individual is: $$u(x_t,\omega_t),$$ where:

  1. $x_t$ is the choice that the individual makes at time $t$.
  2. $\omega_t$ represents variables (events) that the individual does not control (actions of other agents, random shocks to the economy, etc.).
  3. $p_t\bigl(\omega_t \mid x_0,\omega_0,\ldots, x_{t-1}, \omega_{t-1}\bigr)$ is the probability distribution of $\omega_t$, which may depend on the past (choices and events).

Intertemporal Choice: The Standard Model

Discounted Expected Utility Model

We assume the goal of the individual is to choose $\mathbf{x}=(x_0,x_1, \ldots,x_t, \ldots)$ to maximize the discounted expected utility: $$ \max_{\mathbf{x}} \quad\E\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\delta^t\cdot u(x_t,\omega_t)\right],$$ where $0\lt \delta\lt 1$ is the individual's discount factor. That is, we assume the individual is impatient: $u$ units of utility tomorrow are only worth $\delta\cdot u$ units of utility today.

Think of the discounted utility as the analog (for an individual) of the present value of a firm's cash flow.

The symbol $\E$ denotes the expectation, which we use only in cases where there is uncertainty about the future.

Often, the final date is $T=+\infty$ as economic relationships do not come with an “expiration date”.

Behavioral Models of Intertemporal Choice

The $(\beta,\delta)$-model (quasi-hyperbolic discounting)

In the standard model (exponential utility), the discount function is: $$D(t)=\delta^t = \exp \left(-k\cdot t\right),\quad\text{where } k=-\log(\delta).$$

The $(\beta,\delta)$-model uses a different discount function: $$D(t)=\begin{cases} 1 &\text{if } t=0,\\ \beta\cdot \delta^t &\text{if } t\gt 0.\end{cases}$$

  • For streams of utilities that yield the same utility in the present (e.g., $(0,1,0)$ and $(0,0,2)$) both models generate the same results. Why?
  • For streams of utilities that yield different utilities in the present (e.g., $(1,0,0)$ and $(0,2,0)$) their results might differ. Why?
  • The $(\beta,\delta)$-model displays present bias. Why?

Behavioral Models of Intertemporal Choice

Hyperbolic discounting

The hyperbolic discounting function is: $$D(t)=\bigl(1+\alpha\cdot t \bigr)^{-\gamma/\alpha}.$$

  • In the limit, $\alpha\searrow 0$, hyperbolic discounting coincides with exponential discounting. Why?
  • $\lim_{\alpha\searrow 0}\bigl(1+\alpha\cdot t \bigr)^{-\gamma/\alpha}=\exp\left(-\gamma\cdot t\right).$
  • We assume that $0\lt \alpha,\gamma\lt +\infty$.

Dynamic Consistency

Notice that with hyperbolic discounting or with $(\beta,\delta)$ discounting, individual choices might not be dynamically consistent. That is, as time elapses, an individual might change his/her ranking over two flows of utilities even if during the elapsed time both flows delivered the same utility.

Example
Consider $\delta=0.9$ and $\beta=0.1$ and three periods $t=0,1$ and $2$. At period zero we have that $$U^{\beta,\delta}(0,1,0)=0.09\lt U^{\beta,\delta}(0,0,2)=0.081\cdot 2= 0.162.$$ But after period zero has elapsed the preferences are reversed: $$U^{\beta,\delta}(1,0)=1\gt U^{\beta,\delta}(0,2)=0.18.$$

Dynamic Consistency

When time preferences might exhibit dynamically inconsistent behavior, we need to specify how individuals will react. There are three possible alternatives:

  1. The naive approach: the individual does not realize that he/she might change his/her preferences in the future. He/she makes choices in the present period ignoring that his/her plans might be foiled (by himself/herself) in the future.
  2. Strategic approach without commitment: the individual realizes that his/her future self might behave differently than his/her present self wishes, and so he/she will act expecting that her future self will react to her choices.
  3. Strategic approach with commitment: the individual realizes that his/her future self might behave differently than his/her present self wishes, and so he/she will remove the ability of her future self to make choices by committing to the present choices.

A Learning by Doing Exercise

Setup

A $(\beta,\delta)$-individual lives for three periods $t=0,1,2$. In period $t=0$, the individual has two units of “corn”, $I_{0}=2$. At each period, he/she chooses an integer amount of “corn” to consume, $c_{t}$. The remaining “corn” $s_{t}=I_{t}-c_{t}$ is planted and yields $2\cdot s_{t}$ units of “corn” in the next period, $I_{t+1}=2\cdot s_{t}$.

Consumption of $c_{t}$ units of “corn” yields utility $\sqrt{c_{t}}$.

Notice that in the last period $t=2$ there is no point in saving, so $c_{2}=I_{2}$ and $s_{2}=0$. Thus, we only need to find $c_{0}$ and $c_{1}$. Also, notice that $c_{0}$ must be $0$, $1$, or $2$.

A Learning by Doing Exercise

Questions

  1. For each possible value of $c_{0}$, what are the corresponding possible values of $c_{2}$? Hint: if $c_{0}=2$ there is no saving, and therefore income in period $t=1$ is zero, $I_{1}=0$, and so $c_{1}=0$; if $c_{0}=1$ then $s_{0}=1$ and $I_{1}=2$ so $c_{1}$ must be … or … or … (please fill in the blanks and address the case in which $c_{0}=0$).
  2. Using the information above, list all possible eight consumption paths. $(c_{0},c_{1},c_{2})=(2,0,0)$ or $(1,2,0)$ or $(1,1,2)$, etc.
  3. Assuming $\delta=1/2$ and $\beta=3/4$, what is the optimal consumption path from the point of view of a naive (or strategic with commitment power — notice both of them will make the same choices) $(\beta,\delta)$-individual?
