Intertemporal Choice: The Standard Model

Discounted Expected Utility Model

Define $u_t$ as the utility received in period $t$. The discounted utility of a utility flow ${\mathbf{u}=(u_0,u_1,\ldots,u_t,\ldots)}$ is: $$U(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}\delta^{t}\cdot u_t$$

The discount factor $\delta$ is an exogenous parameter that reflects the agent's time preferences: the lower $\delta$, the more impatient the agent. Alternatively, $\delta$ can be interpreted as the probability that the agent survives to the next period.

Intertemporal Choice

General Models of Discounting

Let $D(t)$ be a function of $t$ such that ${D(0)=1}$ and ${0\lt D(t+1)\lt D(t)}$. We call $D(\cdot)$ the discount factor function, and the corresponding discounted utility is: $$U(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}D(t)\cdot u_t$$

Notice that the standard model—also called exponential discounting—is the case in which ${D(t)=\delta^t}$ for ${0\lt\delta\lt 1}$.

Behavioral Models of Intertemporal Choice

The $(\beta,\delta)$-model (quasi-hyperbolic discounting)

In the standard model (exponential utility), the discount function is: $$D(t)=\delta^t = \exp \left(-k\cdot t\right),\quad\text{where } k=-\log(\delta).$$

The $(\beta,\delta)$-model uses a different discount function: $$D(t)=\begin{cases} 1 &\text{if } t=0,\\ \beta\cdot \delta^t &\text{if } t\gt 0.\end{cases}$$

  • For streams of utilities that yield the same utility in the present (e.g., $(0,1,0)$ and $(0,0,2)$) both models generate the same results. Why?
  • For streams of utilities that yield different utilities in the present (e.g., $(1,0,0)$ and $(0,2,0)$) their results might differ. Why?
  • The $(\beta,\delta)$-model displays present bias. Why?

Behavioral Models of Intertemporal Choice

Hyperbolic discounting

The hyperbolic discounting function is: $$D(t)=\bigl(1+\alpha\cdot t \bigr)^{-\gamma/\alpha}$$ where ${\alpha\gt 0}$ and ${\gamma\gt 0}$. Notice that as ${\alpha\searrow 0}$, the discount factor converges to ${D(t)=\exp(-\gamma\cdot t)=\delta^{t}}$, where ${\delta=\exp(-\gamma)}$. Thus, exponential discounting is a limit case of hyperbolic discounting.

Dynamic Consistency

Notice that with hyperbolic discounting or with $(\beta,\delta)$ discounting, individual choices might not be dynamically consistent. That is, as time elapses, an individual might change his/her ranking over two flows of utilities even if during the elapsed time both flows delivered the same utility.

Example
Consider $\delta=0.9$ and $\beta=0.1$ and three periods $t=0,1$ and $2$. At period zero we have that $$U^{\beta,\delta}(0,1,0)=0.09\lt U^{\beta,\delta}(0,0,2)=0.081\cdot 2= 0.162.$$ But after period zero has elapsed the preferences are reversed: $$U^{\beta,\delta}(1,0)=1\gt U^{\beta,\delta}(0,2)=0.18.$$

Dynamic Consistency

When time preferences might exhibit dynamically inconsistent behavior, we need to specify how individuals will react. There are three possible alternatives:

  1. The naive approach: the individual does not realize that he/she might change his/her preferences in the future. He/she makes choices in the present period ignoring that his/her plans might be foiled (by himself/herself) in the future.
  2. Strategic approach without commitment: the individual realizes that his/her future self might behave differently than his/her present self wishes, and so he/she will act expecting that her future self will react to her choices.
  3. Strategic approach with commitment: the individual realizes that his/her future self might behave differently than his/her present self wishes, and so he/she will remove the ability of her future self to make choices by committing to the present choices.

A Learning by Doing Exercise

Setup

A $(\beta,\delta)$-individual lives for three periods $t=0,1,2$ and starts with wealth ${W_{0}=100}$. In each period $t$, the individual consumes $c_{t}$ and saves the rest, ${s_{t}=W_{t}-c_{t}}$. Savings are invested at a 100% rate of return, so next period's wealth is ${W_{t+1}=2\cdot s_{t}}$.

Consuming $c_{t}$ yields utility $\ln c_{t}$.

Notice that in the last period $t=2$ there is no point in saving, so ${c_{2}=W_{2}}$ and ${s_{2}=0}$. Thus, we only need to find $c_{0}$ and $c_{1}$, where $c_{0}$ can be any amount between $0$ and $W_{0}$, and $c_{1}$ any amount between $0$ and $W_{1}$.

A Learning by Doing Exercise

The Naive Approach

  1. Write down the discounted utility as a function of $s_{0}$ and $s_{1}$ only.

    Consumption is ${c_{0}=100-s_{0},}$ ${c_{1}=2s_{0}-s_{1},}$ and ${c_{2}=2s_{1},}$ so $$U_{0}(s_{0},s_{1})=\ln(100-s_{0})+\beta\delta\ln(2s_{0}-s_{1})+\beta\delta^{2}\ln(2s_{1})$$

  2. Write the first-order conditions (FOC) for an interior solution.

    The first-order conditions are: $$\begin{align*} \frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial s_{0}}&=-\frac{1}{100-s_{0}}+\frac{2\beta\delta}{2s_{0}-s_{1}}=0\\ \frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial s_{1}}&=-\frac{\beta\delta}{2s_{0}-s_{1}}+\frac{\beta\delta^{2}}{s_{1}}=0 \end{align*}$$

  3. Solve the FOC for the optimal values of $s_{0}$ and $s_{1}$.

    The second condition gives ${s_{1}=\delta(2s_{0}-s_{1})}$, so ${s_{1}=\frac{2\delta}{1+\delta}\,s_{0}}$ and ${2s_{0}-s_{1}=\frac{2s_{0}}{1+\delta}}$. Substituting into the first condition, ${\frac{1}{100-s_{0}}=\frac{\beta\delta(1+\delta)}{s_{0}}}$, so $$s_{0}^{*}=\frac{100\,\beta\delta(1+\delta)}{1+\beta\delta(1+\delta)}\quad\text{and}\quad s_{1}^{*}=\frac{200\,\beta\delta^{2}}{1+\beta\delta(1+\delta)}$$

A Learning by Doing Exercise

The Strategic Approach without Commitment

Earlier selves of the consumer anticipate the decisions of the future selves.

  1. At time $t=2$, since all wealth is consumed, write consumption
    as a function of savings in the past period.
    $$c_{2}(s_{0},s_{1})=W_{2}=2s_{1}$$
  2. At time $t=1$, write the discounted utility of the present self,
    taking the future self's behavior as given.

    Consumption is ${c_{1}=W_{1}-s_{1}=2s_{0}-s_{1},}$ so $$U_{1}(s_{0},s_{1})=\ln(2s_{0}-s_{1})+\beta\delta\ln(2s_{1})$$

  3. Obtain the corresponding FOC and solve for the optimal $s_{1}$.

    The first-order condition is: $$\frac{\partial U_{1}}{\partial s_{1}}=-\frac{1}{2s_{0}-s_{1}}+\frac{\beta\delta}{s_{1}}=0$$ so ${s_{1}=\beta\delta(2s_{0}-s_{1})}$ and ${\displaystyle s_{1}^{*}(s_{0})=\frac{2\beta\delta}{1+\beta\delta}\,s_{0}}$

  4. At time $t=0$, write the discounted utility of the present self,
    taking the future selves' behavior as given.

    Consumption is ${c_{0}=W_{0}-s_{0}=100-s_{0},}$ so $$\begin{align*} U_{0}(s_{0})&=\ln(100-s_{0})+\beta\delta\ln\bigl(2s_{0}-s_{1}^{*}(s_{0})\bigr)+\beta\delta^{2}\ln\bigl(2s_{1}^{*}(s_{0})\bigr)\\ &=\ln(100-s_{0})+\beta\delta\ln\left(\frac{2s_{0}}{1+\beta\delta}\right)+\beta\delta^{2}\ln\left(\frac{4\beta\delta\,s_{0}}{1+\beta\delta}\right) \end{align*}$$

  5. Obtain the corresponding FOC and solve for the optimal $s_{0}$.

    The first-order condition is: $$\frac{\partial U_{0}}{\partial s_{0}}=-\frac{1}{100-s_{0}}+\frac{\beta\delta}{s_{0}}+\frac{\beta\delta^{2}}{s_{0}}=0$$ so ${s_{0}=\beta\delta(1+\delta)(100-s_{0})}$ and ${\displaystyle s_{0}^{*}=\frac{100\,\beta\delta(1+\delta)}{1+\beta\delta(1+\delta)}}$

  6. Use the optimal value of $s_{0}$ to write the optimal $s_{1}$
    as a function of $\beta$ and $\delta$ only.

    Substituting $s_{0}^{*}$ into $s_{1}^{*}(s_{0})$: $$s_{1}^{*}=\frac{2\beta\delta}{1+\beta\delta}\,s_{0}^{*}=\frac{200\,\beta^{2}\delta^{2}(1+\delta)}{(1+\beta\delta)\bigl(1+\beta\delta(1+\delta)\bigr)}$$

A Learning by Doing Exercise

The Strategic Approach with Commitment

If the naive solution were implemented, the agent would save ${s_{0}=\frac{100\,\beta\delta(1+\delta)}{1+\beta\delta(1+\delta)}}$ in period $0$ and ${s_{1}=\frac{200\,\beta\delta^{2}}{1+\beta\delta(1+\delta)}}$ in period $1$, and the utility would be: $$U_{0}^{\text{ideal}}=\ln\left(\frac{100}{1+\beta\delta(1+\delta)}\right)+\beta\delta\ln\left(\frac{200\,\beta\delta}{1+\beta\delta(1+\delta)}\right)+\beta\delta^{2}\ln\left(\frac{400\,\beta\delta^{2}}{1+\beta\delta(1+\delta)}\right)$$ But in period $t=1$, the agent would not choose according to the naive solution; the agent changes its mind and chooses: $$s_{1}=\frac{\beta+\beta\delta}{1+\beta\delta}\cdot\frac{200\,\beta\delta^{2}}{1+\beta\delta(1+\delta)}$$ So the actual utility of the naive agent ends up being: $$U_{0}^{\text{actual}}=\ln\left(\frac{100}{1+\beta\delta(1+\delta)}\right)+\beta\delta\ln\left(\frac{200\,\beta\delta(1+\delta)}{(1+\beta\delta)\bigl(1+\beta\delta(1+\delta)\bigr)}\right)+\beta\delta^{2}\ln\left(\frac{400\,\beta^{2}\delta^{2}(1+\delta)}{(1+\beta\delta)\bigl(1+\beta\delta(1+\delta)\bigr)}\right)$$ which is lower than the utility of the naive solution: $$U_{0}^{\text{ideal}}-U_{0}^{\text{actual}}=\beta\delta\left[(1+\delta)\ln\left(\frac{1+\beta\delta}{1+\delta}\right)-\delta\ln\beta\right]\gt 0$$

If the agent has a commitment device that forces the naive solution to be chosen in period $t=1$, then, as long as the cost of the commitment device is not too high, the agent will commit to the naive solution.

Procrastination

The Problem

An individual must complete a task exactly once, and completing it costs $26$. Until the task is completed, the individual bears a loss in every single period: the loss in period $t$ is ${L_{t}=t+1,}$ which grows the longer the task is put off. So if the task is completed in period ${\tau,}$ the individual bears the loss $L_{t}$ in each period ${t=0,1,\ldots,\tau-1,}$ pays $26$ in period ${\tau,}$ and bears nothing afterwards.

Costs are negative utilities: ${u_{t}=-L_{t}}$ for ${t\lt\tau,}$ ${u_{\tau}=-26,}$ and ${u_{t}=0}$ for ${t\gt\tau.}$ Throughout, ${\delta=0.8}$ and ${\beta=0.5.}$

In which period does the individual complete the task?

Procrastination

The Exponential Agent

Seen from period ${t,}$ with the task still pending, the cost of completing it in period ${\tau\ge t}$ is: $$C_{t}(\tau)=\sum_{s=t}^{\tau-1}\delta^{s-t}L_{s}+\delta^{\tau-t}\cdot 26$$ Postponing the task from $\tau$ to $\tau+1$ changes this cost by: $$C_{t}(\tau+1)-C_{t}(\tau)=\delta^{\tau-t}\bigl(L_{\tau}-(1-\delta)\cdot 26\bigr)$$ Postponing adds the loss $L_{\tau}$ but saves ${(1-\delta)\cdot 26=5.2,}$ the gain from paying $26$ one period later. Since $L_{\tau}$ grows over time, the agent completes the task in the first period in which ${L_{\tau}\ge 5.2.}$ This period does not depend on ${t,}$ so the exponential agent never changes its mind.

Since ${L_{4}=5\lt 5.2\lt 6=L_{5},}$ the exponential agent completes the task in period ${\tau=5.}$

Procrastination

The Sophisticated Agent

For a $(\beta,\delta)$-agent in period ${t,}$ with the task still pending, completing the task now costs ${26,}$ while waiting until period ${\tau\gt t}$ costs: $$C_{t}^{\beta}(\tau)=L_{t}+\beta\Bigl[\sum_{s=t+1}^{\tau-1}\delta^{s-t}L_{s}+\delta^{\tau-t}\cdot 26\Bigr]$$ A sophisticated agent knows that its future selves are also present-biased, so it solves the problem backward. From period $15$ on, every self completes the task, since waiting costs at least ${L_{t}+\beta\delta\cdot 26=(t+1)+10.4\gt 26.}$ Working backward from there, each self waits until the next self that completes the task. For example, self $13$ expects self $15$ to complete it, so waiting costs ${C_{13}^{\beta}(15)=14+0.5\,(0.8\cdot 15+0.64\cdot 26)=28.32\gt 26.}$

Procrastination

The Naive Agent

A naive agent believes that its future selves will carry out whatever plan the current self makes. Because $\beta$ multiplies all future costs equally, the best plan for later is the exponential agent’s: complete the task in period $5$ if ${t\lt 5,}$ or in period $t+1$ if ${t\ge 5.}$

Before period ${5,}$ waiting is cheaper even for the exponential agent, so the naive agent waits. From period $5$ on, the naive agent completes the task now only if ${26\le L_{t}+\beta\delta\cdot 26,}$ that is, if ${L_{t}\ge(1-\beta\delta)\cdot 26=15.6.}$ Since ${L_{14}=15\lt 15.6\lt 16=L_{15},}$ the naive agent completes the task in period ${\tau=15.}$ In every period from $5$ to ${14,}$ it plans to complete the task next period and then postpones again.

A general discounting formulation: $$D(t)=\begin{cases} 1 &\text{if } t=0,\\ \prod_{k=1}^{t}\delta_k &\text{if } t\gt 0,\end{cases}$$ where ${1\gt\delta_{k+1}\ge\delta_k\gt 0}$.

Notice that $\delta_t$ is not the discount factor applied to the period-$t$ utility, ${D(t).}$

Examples:

  • Exponential discounting: ${\delta_k=\delta.}$
  • Quasi-hyperbolic discounting: ${\delta_1=\beta\cdot\delta}$ and ${\delta_k=\delta}$ for ${k\gt 1.}$
  • Hyperbolic discounting: ${\delta_k=\left(\frac{1+\alpha\cdot k-\alpha}{1+\alpha\cdot k}\right)^{\gamma/\alpha}.}$

Delay in Q1 and No Delay in Q2

Let $v(x)=x$ be the utility of receiving ${\$x,}$ and measure time in days from today. Let $T$ be June 17th, 2029, so that June 16th, 2029 is day $T-1$ and June 17th, 2028 is day ${T-365.}$ We show that no discount function ${D(t)=\prod_{k=1}^{t}\delta_k}$ with ${1\gt\delta_{k+1}\ge\delta_k\gt 0}$ makes the delay (B) the best choice in Q1 and no delay (A) the best choice in Q2.

B is the best choice in Q1. Divide both sides by ${D(T-365):}$

$$607.07\cdot D(T)\ge 467\cdot D(T-365)\iff\prod_{k=T-364}^{T}\delta_k\ge\frac{467}{607.07}.$$

A is the best choice in Q2. Divide both sides by ${D(T-1):}$

$$467\cdot D(T-1)\ge 467.39\cdot D(T)\iff\delta_T\le\frac{467}{467.39}.$$

Rubinstein (2003)

A Contradiction

Since $\delta_k\le\delta_T$ for every ${k\le T,}$ replacing each $\delta_k$ in the product by $\delta_T$ can only raise it, so the inequality for Q1 still holds:

$$\delta_T^{365}\ge\prod_{k=T-364}^{T}\delta_k\ge\frac{467}{607.07}\iff\left(\frac{1}{\delta_T}\right)^{365}\le\frac{607.07}{467}.$$

The inequality for Q2 gives ${1/\delta_T\ge 467.39/467=1+0.39/467.}$ Since ${(1+x)^n\ge 1+n\cdot x}$ for ${x\ge 0}$ (expand the product):

$$\left(\frac{1}{\delta_T}\right)^{365}\ge\left(1+\frac{0.39}{467}\right)^{365}\ge 1+\frac{365\cdot 0.39}{467}=\frac{609.35}{467}.$$

Together:

$$\frac{609.35}{467}\le\left(\frac{1}{\delta_T}\right)^{365}\le\frac{607.07}{467},$$

that is, ${609.35\le 607.07,}$ a contradiction. So delay in Q1 together with no delay in Q2 is impossible.

Preferences Over Utility Streams

In every discounting model the discount function $D(t)$ is decreasing in $t$, so an individual who discounts the future always prefers to receive gains sooner and to postpone losses.

That prediction contradicts factual evidence. Often, “when choosing between sequences of events, people will make a point of scheduling the unpleasant experience first and the pleasant one later” (Angner, A Course in Behavioral Economics, 2nd ed., Palgrave Macmillan, 2016, p. 207).

An alternative to discounting is to evaluate a utility profile with the peak-end rule.

Peak-End Rule

Let ${\mathbf{u}=(u_0,u_1,\dots,u_T)}$ be a utility profile, and let $f(x,y)$ be strictly increasing in both arguments. A peak-end rule evaluates $\mathbf{u}$ as $$U(\mathbf{u})=f\Bigl(\max_{t} u_t,\;u_T\Bigr).$$ Only two numbers matter: the peak utility $\max_{t} u_t$ and the final utility $u_T$. The rest of the profile is ignored.
