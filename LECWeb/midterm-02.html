Question 1

Question 1

“My friend Jeffrey and I were given two tickets to a professional basketball game in Buffalo, normally a 75-minute drive from Rochester. On the day of the game there was a snowstorm and we sensibly decided to skip the game. But Jeffrey, who is not an economist, remarked ‘if we had paid full price for those tickets we would have gone!’”

Richard H. Thaler, “From Cashews to Nudges: The Evolution of Behavioral Economics,” Nobel Prize Lecture, December 8, 2017, p. 489

Choose the BEST pairings. When deciding between A) driving to the game and B) skipping the game:

  • Uses the price paid for the tickets as a reference point
  • Compares the utility of total wealth plus the monetary value of watching the game and driving 75 minutes through a snowstorm with the utility of total wealth plus the corresponding opportunity cost
  • Ignores sunk costs

Choices: budgeting, comprehensive account, hedonic editing, minimal account, present bias, topical account.

Answer 1
StatementAccount
Uses the price paid for the tickets as a reference pointTopical account
Compares the utility of total wealth plus … with the utility of total wealth plus the corresponding opportunity costComprehensive account
Ignores sunk costsMinimal account

Recall the three ways of framing outcomes.

  • Topical account. It relates the consequences of a choice to a reference level set by the context. Jeffrey’s remark makes the price paid that reference: had they paid full price, skipping the game would have felt like losing that money, so they would have gone.
  • Comprehensive account. It evaluates each option by its effect on total wealth: total wealth plus the game and the drive through the snowstorm, against total wealth plus whatever else they could do with the evening. This is the comprehensive account of the jacket and calculator example.
  • Minimal account. It compares the options by their differences only, ignoring everything they have in common. The price of the tickets is the same whether they drive or stay home, so it drops out: the minimal account ignores sunk costs.

The three unused choices.

  • Hedonic editing. It is about combining two or more outcomes: segregate or integrate them; see Hedonic Editing. None of the statements combines outcomes.
  • Budgeting. It assigns spending to categories, sometimes held to budgets; see The Three Components of Mental Accounting. The statements compare two options, not categories of spending.
  • Present bias. It belongs to intertemporal choice: the present gets extra weight; see the $(\beta,\delta)$-model. Both options play out on the same evening.

Question 3

Question 3

Pick the probability weighting function that best showcases the assumptions of Prospect Theory.

  • $\pi(0.1)=0.1\lt\pi(0.3)=0.3\lt\pi(0.7)=0.7\lt\pi(0.9)=0.9$
  • $\pi(0)=0.1\lt\pi(0.3)=0.5\lt\pi(0.9)=0.85\lt\pi(1)=1$
  • $\pi(0.1)=0.05\lt\pi(0.3)=0.2\lt\pi(0.7)=0.8\lt\pi(0.9)=0.95$
  • $\pi(0)=0\lt\pi(0.3)=0.5\lt\pi(0.9)=0.85\lt\pi(1)=1$
  • $\pi(0.1)=0.2\lt\pi(0.3)=0.5\lt\pi(0.7)=0.85\lt\pi(0.9)=0.95$
  • $\pi(0)=0\lt\pi(0.3)=0.5\lt\pi(0.9)=0.85\lt\pi(1)=0.9$
  • $\pi(0.1)=0.05\lt\pi(0.3)=0.15\lt\pi(0.7)=0.5\lt\pi(0.9)=0.75$
  • $\pi(0)=0\lt\pi(0.3)=0.5\gt\pi(0.9)=0.45\lt\pi(1)=1$
  • $\pi(0)=0.1\lt\pi(0.3)=0.5\lt\pi(0.9)=0.85\lt\pi(1)=0.9$
Answer 3

Answer. $\pi(0)=0\lt\pi(0.3)=0.5\lt\pi(0.9)=0.85\lt\pi(1)=1$.

Recall the decision weights of Prospect Theory: they overestimate unlikely events, $\pi(p)\gt p$ for small $p$; they underestimate likely events, with $\pi(p)+\pi(1-p)\le 1$; and $\pi(p)=p$ only at $p=0$, $\hat p$, and $1$. They also increase with $p$: a likelier event never weighs less.

  • $\pi(0.1)=0.1\lt\pi(0.3)=0.3\lt\pi(0.7)=0.7\lt\pi(0.9)=0.9$. Incorrect. Every weight equals its probability: this is expected utility theory, with no distortion at all.
  • $\pi(0)=0.1\lt\cdots\lt\pi(1)=1$. Incorrect. An impossible event gets weight $0.1$; Prospect Theory has $\pi(0)=0$.
  • $\pi(0.1)=0.05\lt\pi(0.3)=0.2\lt\pi(0.7)=0.8\lt\pi(0.9)=0.95$. Incorrect. Prospect Theory in reverse: the unlikely event is underweighted, $\pi(0.1)=0.05\lt 0.1$, and the likely one overweighted, $\pi(0.9)=0.95\gt 0.9$.
  • $\pi(0)=0\lt\pi(0.3)=0.5\lt\pi(0.9)=0.85\lt\pi(1)=1$. Correct. It shows every assumption at once: the impossible event gets no weight and the certain event full weight, the unlikely event is overweighted, $\pi(0.3)=0.5\gt 0.3$, the likely event is underweighted, $\pi(0.9)=0.85\lt 0.9$, and the weights increase.
  • $\pi(0.1)=0.2\lt\pi(0.3)=0.5\lt\pi(0.7)=0.85\lt\pi(0.9)=0.95$. Incorrect. Likely events are overweighted too, $\pi(0.9)=0.95\gt 0.9$, and $\pi(0.3)+\pi(0.7)=1.35\gt 1$ breaks $\pi(p)+\pi(1-p)\le 1$.
  • $\pi(0)=0\lt\cdots\lt\pi(1)=0.9$. Incorrect. A certain event loses weight; Prospect Theory has $\pi(1)=1$.
  • $\pi(0.1)=0.05\lt\pi(0.3)=0.15\lt\pi(0.7)=0.5\lt\pi(0.9)=0.75$. Incorrect. Unlikely events are underweighted too, $\pi(0.1)=0.05\lt 0.1$, against $\pi(p)\gt p$ for small $p$.
  • $\pi(0)=0\lt\pi(0.3)=0.5\gt\pi(0.9)=0.45\lt\pi(1)=1$. Incorrect. The weights fall from $\pi(0.3)=0.5$ to $\pi(0.9)=0.45$: a likelier event weighs less, which Prospect Theory rules out.
  • $\pi(0)=0.1\lt\cdots\lt\pi(1)=0.9$. Incorrect. Both endpoints are wrong: the impossible event gets weight and the certain event loses some.

Questions 6 to 9

You have just started a new job at a company with 90 employees. At the holiday party, the company will draw one employee’s name at random, and you may bet on the department of the employee drawn. You know that 30 employees work in Sales. Each of the other 60 works in either Marketing or IT, but as a newcomer you have no idea how they split: any number of them, from 0 to 60, may work in Marketing.

Each lottery below pays $900 if its event happens and nothing otherwise.

LotteryWins if the employee drawn works in
SSales
MMarketing
SISales or IT
MIMarketing or IT

You face two decision problems: choose between S and M, and choose between SI and MI.

You are ambiguity averse and use max-min expected utility: the value of a lottery is its lowest expected utility over all possible splits of the 60 employees, and you choose the lottery with the highest value. Your utility of money is $u(x)=x$.

Below, $m$ is the number of Marketing employees, $m\in\{0,1,\ldots,60\}$; see Max Min Expected Utility Theory.

Question 6
The max-min expected utility of lottery S is:
Answer 6

Answer. $300$.

Sales has 30 of the 90 employees whatever the split, so the expected utility is the same for every $m$:

$$\tfrac{30}{90}\cdot 900=300.$$
Question 7
The max-min expected utility of lottery M is:
Answer 7

Answer. $0$.

With $m$ employees in Marketing, the expected utility is

$$\tfrac{m}{90}\cdot 900=10m,$$

which is lowest at $m=0$, when nobody works in Marketing.

Question 8
The max-min expected utility of lottery SI is:
Answer 8

Answer. $300$.

Sales or IT has $30+(60-m)=90-m$ employees, so the expected utility is

$$\tfrac{90-m}{90}\cdot 900=10(90-m),$$

which is lowest at $m=60$, when nobody works in IT: $10\cdot 30=300$.

Question 9
The max-min expected utility of lottery MI is:
Answer 9

Answer. $600$.

Marketing or IT has 60 of the 90 employees whatever the split:

$$\tfrac{60}{90}\cdot 900=600.$$

Putting the four together, you choose S over M ($300\gt 0$) and MI over SI ($600\gt 300$): the Ellsberg pattern. No single belief about $m$ produces it with expected utility: S beats M only if $300\gt 10m$, that is, $m\lt 30$, while MI beats SI only if $600\gt 10(90-m)$, that is, $m\gt 30$.

Questions 12 to 14

You are a freelance designer, and you have accepted a commission that is due tomorrow. You can do the work today or on any later day. Doing it takes a full day of effort, which costs you 60 on the day you do it, and you submit the work that same day. The client pays you 100 the day after you submit. The contract, however, has a late penalty: for each day past the deadline that you submit, the payment falls by 10. For example, if you do the work today or tomorrow, there is no penalty, but if you do it the day after tomorrow, you get only 90, paid the following day. If you never do the work, you bear no cost and receive no payment.

Costs and payments are measured in utility.

Question 12
Assume the standard model (exponential discounting) with $\delta=0.9$. What is the utility of submitting today?
Answer 12

Answer. $30$.

The cost of 60 falls today; the payment of 100 arrives tomorrow, one period away:

$$-60+0.9\cdot 100=-60+90=30.$$
Question 13
Assume the standard model (exponential discounting) with $\delta=0.9$. What is the utility, as of today, of submitting tomorrow, on the deadline?
Answer 13

Answer. $27$.

The cost falls tomorrow; the full payment of 100 arrives the day after:

$$0.9\cdot(-60)+0.9^2\cdot 100=-54+81=27.$$
Question 14
Assume the standard model (exponential discounting) with $\delta=0.9$. What is the utility, as of today, of submitting one day after the deadline?
Answer 14

Answer. $17.01$.

The cost falls the day after tomorrow; the payment, cut to 90, arrives the day after that:

$$0.9^2\cdot(-60)+0.9^3\cdot 90=-48.6+65.61=17.01.$$

Since $30\gt 27\gt 17.01$, and later days are worth even less, the exponential agent submits today; see The Exponential Agent.

Questions 15 to 17

The setting of Questions 12 to 14. In the $(\beta,\delta)$-model, today’s utility is not discounted, and every future utility is multiplied by $\beta\,\delta^t$.

Question 15
Assume a naive quasi-hyperbolic ($\beta$-$\delta$) agent with $\beta=0.7$ and $\delta=0.9$. What is the utility of submitting today?
Answer 15

Answer. $3$.

The cost falls today, undiscounted; the payment arrives tomorrow, discounted by $\beta\delta=0.63$:

$$-60+0.7\cdot 0.9\cdot 100=-60+63=3.$$
Question 16
Assume a naive quasi-hyperbolic ($\beta$-$\delta$) agent with $\beta=0.7$ and $\delta=0.9$. What is the utility, as of today, of submitting tomorrow, on the deadline?
Answer 16

Answer. $18.9$.

Both the cost and the payment are in the future, so $\beta$ multiplies both, and the answer is $\beta$ times the answer to Question 13:

$$0.7\cdot\bigl(0.9\cdot(-60)+0.9^2\cdot 100\bigr)=0.7\cdot 27=18.9.$$
Question 17
Assume a naive quasi-hyperbolic ($\beta$-$\delta$) agent with $\beta=0.7$ and $\delta=0.9$. What is the utility, as of today, of submitting one day after the deadline?
Answer 17

Answer. $11.907$.

Again $\beta$ times the exponential answer, here the answer to Question 14:

$$0.7\cdot\bigl(0.9^2\cdot(-60)+0.9^3\cdot 90\bigr)=0.7\cdot 17.01=11.907.$$

Question 19

The setting of Questions 12 to 17.

Question 19

Assume a naive quasi-hyperbolic ($\beta$-$\delta$) agent with $\beta=0.7$ and $\delta=0.9$. Label your answers 1) and 2); otherwise, you may lose points.

1) On which day does the agent plan to submit the work?

2) When that day arrives, does the agent submit the work? Why or why not?

Answer 19

1) Tomorrow, on the deadline. As of today, submitting tomorrow is worth $18.9$, more than submitting today ($3$), one day late ($11.907$), or never ($0$). Later days are worth even less.

2) No. When tomorrow comes, the agent discounts from tomorrow’s point of view. Submitting now is worth $-60+0.7\cdot 0.9\cdot 100=3$, while submitting the next day, one day late, looks worth

$$0.7\cdot\bigl(0.9\cdot(-60)+0.9^2\cdot 90\bigr)=0.7\cdot 18.9=13.23.$$

Since $13.23\gt 3$, the agent postpones again. The same happens every day after that:

DaySubmit nowSubmit the next day
Tomorrow (deadline)$3$$13.23$
1 day late$-3.3$$7.56$
2 days late$-9.6$$1.89$
3 days late$-15.9$$-3.78$

Each day the agent plans to submit the next day, and each day it postpones again. Three days late, even the next day is worth less than never doing the work ($0$), so the naive agent never submits; see The Naive Agent.

Question 20

Question 20

Suppose your value function is $v(x)=\operatorname{sign}(x)\sqrt{\lvert x\rvert}$, that is, $v(x)=\sqrt{x}$ for gains and $v(x)=-\sqrt{\lvert x\rvert}$ for losses. For example, $v(4)=2$ and $v(-4)=-2$. So you are risk averse over gains and risk loving over losses, but not loss averse.

Two events happen: X, a loss of 900, and Y, a gain of 0.04. Is it better to segregate or to integrate X and Y? Why? Show your work, using at least two decimal places.

Answer 20

Answer. Segregate.

$$\text{Integrate: }\ v(-900+0.04)=-\sqrt{899.96}\approx -29.9993\approx -30.00$$ $$\text{Segregate: }\ v(-900)+v(0.04)=-30+0.2=-29.80$$

Since $-29.80\gt -30.00$, segregating is better.

Why: on the gain side, $v$ is steepest at the origin, while far into the losses it is almost flat. Segregated, the tiny gain is worth $v(0.04)=0.2$. Integrated, it only shrinks a loss of 900 to 899.96, which raises the value by about $0.04\cdot v'(-900)=0.04/60\approx 0.0007$. This is the silver-lining rule of hedonic editing: segregate small gains from larger losses.

Question 22

Question 22

Consider the hedonic editing hypothesis, $v(x\,\&\,y)=\max\{v(x+y),\,v(x)+v(y)\}$, where $v$ is risk averse over gains, risk loving over losses, and displays loss aversion. According to Thaler, for what type of events $x$ and $y$ did subjects in the experiments report $v(x\,\&\,y)=\min\{v(x+y),\,v(x)+v(y)\}$, contrary to the hypothesis?

  • $x$ and $y$ are both gains
  • $x$ is a small gain and $y$ is a large loss
  • For every type of events: subjects never edited as the hypothesis predicts
  • $x$ is a gain and $y$ is a loss of the same size
  • $x$ and $y$ are both losses
  • $x$ is a large gain and $y$ is a small loss
  • $x$ and $y$ are any mix of one gain and one loss
  • For no type of events: subjects always edited as the hypothesis predicts
Answer 22

Answer. $x$ and $y$ are both losses.

Thaler (1999, p. 188), on the experiments of Thaler and Johnson (1990): “For gains, the hedonic editing hypothesis was supported. … But, in contrast to the hedonic editing hypothesis, subjects thought separating losses was also a good idea.” Since $v$ is convex over losses, two losses are worth more together, $v(x+y)\gt v(x)+v(y)$: the hypothesis says integrate them. Subjects chose to separate them, $v(x)+v(y)$, the minimum. In Thaler’s words, they feel “that losses must be felt one by one, and that bearing one loss makes one more sensitive to the next.”

  • Both gains. Incorrect. For gains the hypothesis was supported: subjects preferred to separate gains, and since $v$ is concave over gains, $v(x)+v(y)$ is the maximum.
  • A small gain and a large loss. Incorrect. A silver lining: the hypothesis says segregate, and subjects agreed. People “actively parse outcomes consistent with these rules, with the exception of multiple losses.”
  • Every type of events. Incorrect. For gains the hypothesis was supported.
  • A gain and a loss of the same size. Incorrect. A mixed pair; only multiple losses broke the rules.
  • Both losses. Correct. See above.
  • A large gain and a small loss. Incorrect. The hypothesis says integrate the smaller loss with the larger gain, and people do; only multiple losses broke the rules.
  • Any mix of one gain and one loss. Incorrect. Mixed pairs were consistent with the rules; only multiple losses were not.
  • No type of events. Incorrect. Multiple losses broke the hypothesis.

Question 23

Question 23

Suppose your value function is $v(x)=\operatorname{sign}(x)\sqrt{\lvert x\rvert}$, and you weight outcomes by their probabilities. Hint: if you don’t have a calculator, use $\sqrt{1000}=\sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{500}$ and $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\lt 1$.

Problem 1. In addition to whatever you own, you have been given 1000. Choose between (A) a 50% chance of winning 1000 and (B) winning 500 for sure.

Problem 2. In addition to whatever you own, you have been given 2000. Choose between (C) a 50% chance of losing 1000 and (D) losing 500 for sure.

Assume you integrate the gift into your reference point. Label your answers 1) to 3); otherwise, you may lose points.

1) Find the values of A and B. Which do you choose in Problem 1?

2) Find the values of C and D. Which do you choose in Problem 2?

3) In final outcomes, A is the same as C, and B is the same as D. Why do your choices differ?

Answer 23

1) B.

$$v(A)=\tfrac12\sqrt{1000}=\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\sqrt{500}\approx 15.81,\qquad v(B)=\sqrt{500}\approx 22.36.$$

Since $\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\lt 1$, $v(A)\lt v(B)$ even without a calculator.

2) C.

$$v(C)=-\tfrac12\sqrt{1000}=-\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\sqrt{500}\approx -15.81,\qquad v(D)=-\sqrt{500}\approx -22.36.$$

3) The gift moves the reference point, so the same final outcomes are coded differently. A and C both leave you with 2000 or 1000, with equal chances; B and D both leave you with 1500 for sure. In Problem 1 the reference point is 1000, so the outcomes are gains, where $v$ is concave: risk averse, you take the sure thing. In Problem 2 the reference point is 2000, so the outcomes are losses, where $v$ is convex: risk loving, you take the gamble. Same final outcomes, different frames, different choices: a framing effect. Compare Practice Problem 6, Question 2.

Question 24

Question 24
Professor Lerath’s students take Behavioral Economics Midterm 2 one question at a time, in order. They prefer easy questions to hard ones, and between two questions of equal difficulty, they prefer the one worth more points. Professor Lerath wants to maximize the students’ utility and always ends the exam with an easy question worth many points. What rule does Professor Lerath believe the students use to evaluate the stream of utility they experience during the exam? Explain.
Answer 24

Answer. The peak-end rule.

Under the peak-end rule, the exam is evaluated as $U(\mathbf{u})=f\bigl(\max_t u_t,\,u_T\bigr)$: only the best moment and the last one count. An easy question worth many points is the students’ favorite kind, so ending with it makes the final utility $u_T$ as high as it can be, and it is the peak as well.

A professor who believed the students discount, exponentially or with the $(\beta,\delta)$-model, would do the opposite: the discount function is decreasing, so the best question should come first. Also accepted: a preference for improving sequences, “saving the best for last.”
