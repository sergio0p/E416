Game Theory

A Quick Review

The strategy profile $a_{-i}$ is a list of actions of players distinct from player $i$. The best response of player $i$ is the set of all actions $x$ that maximize $u_i(x,a_{-i})$: the payoff of player $i$ when the others play according to $a_{-i}$. We write $BR_i(a_{-i})$ for the best response function of player $i$.

A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile $s=(s_1,\ldots,s_n)$ such that $s_i=BR_i(s_{-i})$ — every player is best responding to the others' actions.

Golden Balls

Nash Equilibrium

of Games with a Continuum of Actions

Consider a two-player game in which player 1 chooses $x$, player 2 chooses $y$, and the payoffs are $u_1(x,y)$ and $u_2(x,y)$. Assuming that marginal utilities of one's own action are decreasing, we obtain player 1's best response function by solving: $$ \diff{}{x}u_1(x,y)=0$$ and player 2's best response by solving $$\diff{ }{y}u_2(x,y)=0.$$ To obtain the Nash equilibria we solve the system of equations given by the best response functions: $$x=BR_1(y),\qquad y=BR_2(x).$$

Dynamic Games

In dynamic games, a strategy of a player prescribes the action that the player will play at each node in the game tree in which the player moves. We focus attention on subgame perfect Nash equilibrium, which is a Nash equilibrium in which at every node of the tree (including nodes that might not be reached during play) players are always best responding.

Continuation

Player 1 anticipates player 2's best response and chooses $x$ to maximize his/her payoff given player 2's best response: $$u_1(x,BR_2(x)),$$ so player 1's best response is found by solving $$\ddiff{}{x}u_1(x,BR_2(x))=\diff{}{x}u_1(x,BR_2(x))+\diff{}{y}u_1(x,BR_2(x))\cdot \ddiff{BR_2(x)}{x}=0.$$

Public Good Contribution

A player has to divide one dollar between private consumption $1-x_i$ and donation to a public good $x_i$. Notice that for public goods, consumption is non-rival. The payoffs are: $$u_i(x)=1-x_i+\gamma\cdot \sum_{j=1}^n x_j,$$ where $1/n\lt \gamma\lt 1$ and $n$ is the number of players.

Notice that since $\diff{u_i}{x_i}=-1+\gamma\lt 0$, we have $BR_i(x_{-i})=0$ but $u_i(0,\ldots,0)=1\lt u_i(1,\ldots,1)=\gamma\cdot n$.

The Ultimatum Game

The Ultimatum Game
Player 1 makes demand $x$ and Player 2, after observing it, says either yes or no. Payoffs are: $u_1(x,y)=x$, $u_2(x,y)=1-x$, and $u_1(x,n)=u_2(x,n)=0.$
  • Player 2's best response is to say yes to any $x\lt 1$.
  • Player 2 is indifferent between saying yes or no to $x=1$.

The Ultimatum Game

  • Thus player 2 has two strategies that can be part of a subgame perfect equilibrium: $$s^*(x)=y\text{ for all } x\quad\text{ and }\quad s^{**}(x)=\begin{cases}y\,\text{ if } &x\lt 1,\\ n\,\text{ if } &x=1.\end{cases}$$
  • In any subgame perfect equilibrium: player 1 demands the whole pie and player 2 always says yes, $(1,s^{*})$.
  • But in experiments (in market economies), offers below 20% are often rejected; also, offers below 20% are rare.

Behavioral Game Theory Models

Social Preferences

Let $\mathbf{u}=(u_1,\ldots,u_n)$ be the payoff profile of a game with $n$ players. Behavioral game theory assumes that players have social preferences: preferences over entire payoff profiles $\mathbf{u}$. Player $i$ does not value her own payoff $u_i$ in isolation; she cares about how every player fares, herself included. Her utility $U_i(\mathbf{u})$ represents these social preferences. Some examples:

Selfish
$U_i(\mathbf{u})=u_i$: only her own payoff matters (the standard assumption).
Altruistic
$U_i(\mathbf{u})=\dfrac{u_i+\lambda\sum_{j\neq i}u_j}{1+\lambda(n-1)}$, with $\lambda\gt 0$: the other players' payoffs raise her utility.
Envious
$U_i(\mathbf{u})=u_i-\lambda\sum_{j\neq i}u_j$, with $\lambda\gt 0$: the other players' payoffs lower her utility.
Utilitarian
$U_i(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{j=1}^n u_j$: the greatest good for the greatest number.
Rawlsian
$U_i(\mathbf{u})=\min\{u_1,\ldots,u_n\}$: only the worst-off player's payoff matters.
Inequality averse
$U_i(\mathbf{u})=-\left(\max_j u_j-\min_j u_j\right)$: she dislikes the gap between the best and the worst off.

Fehr and Schmidt (1999)

Inequity aversion model ($\beta_i\le \alpha_i$ and $0\le \beta_i\lt 1$): $$U_i(\mathbf{u})=u_i-\frac{\alpha_i}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i} \underbrace{\max(u_j-u_i,0)}_{\text{envy}}-\frac{\beta_i}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i} \underbrace{\max(u_i-u_j,0)}_{\text{guilt}}.$$

The Ultimatum Game under Social Preferences

To find the subgame perfect equilibrium (the credible equilibrium), we solve the game backwards, from the last round to the first. First, let's consider the subgames in which Player 1 chose $x\gt\tfrac{1}{2}$ in the first round.

Player 2 prefers to say yes if and only if $$\begin{align*} &1-x-\alpha(2x-1)\ge 0\quad\Leftrightarrow\\ &x\le\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha} \end{align*}$$

Notice that the largest demand Player 2 is willing to accept, $\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha}$, decreases with $\alpha$: it equals $1$ when $\alpha=0$ and falls toward $\tfrac{1}{2}$ as $\alpha\to\infty$.

Next, let's consider the subgames in which Player 1 chose $x\lt\tfrac{1}{2}$ in the first round.

Player 2 prefers to say yes if and only if $$\begin{align*} &1-x-\beta(1-2x)\ge 0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad(2\beta-1)\,x\ge\beta-1\\ &x\le\frac{1-\beta}{1-2\beta}\ \text{ if }\beta\lt\tfrac{1}{2},\qquad x\ge\frac{\beta-1}{2\beta-1}\ \text{ if }\beta\gt\tfrac{1}{2} \end{align*}$$

Since $\beta\lt 1$, every demand $x\lt\tfrac{1}{2}$ satisfies this inequality, so Player 2 accepts it. Without the assumption $\beta\lt 1$, however, overly generous offers (small demands $x$) could be rejected: if $\beta\gt 1$, Player 2 rejects every demand $x\lt\frac{\beta-1}{2\beta-1}$.

Now, given Player 2's best response in the last round, Player 1's payoff from choosing $x$ in the first round is $$u_1(x,BR_2(x))=\begin{cases} x-\alpha(1-2x) & \text{if } x\lt\tfrac{1}{2}\\ x-\beta(2x-1) & \text{if } \tfrac{1}{2}\le x\le\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha}\\ 0 & \text{if } x\gt\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha} \end{cases}$$

Notice that $$\dfrac{d}{dx}u_1=\begin{cases} 1+2\alpha & \text{if } x\lt\tfrac{1}{2}\\ 1-2\beta & \text{if } \tfrac{1}{2}\lt x\lt\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha}\\ 0 & \text{if } x\gt\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha} \end{cases}$$

In the range $x\lt\tfrac{1}{2}$, Player 1 always wants to increase $x$. In the range $\tfrac{1}{2}\lt x\lt\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha}$, Player 1 has an incentive to increase $x$ if $\beta\lt\tfrac{1}{2}$ and to decrease it if $\beta\gt\tfrac{1}{2}$. In sum, $$BR_1=\begin{cases} \frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha} & \text{if } \beta\lt\tfrac{1}{2}\\ \text{any } x\in\left[\tfrac{1}{2},\frac{1+\alpha}{1+2\alpha}\right] & \text{if } \beta=\tfrac{1}{2}\\ \tfrac{1}{2} & \text{if } \beta\gt\tfrac{1}{2} \end{cases}$$

The Dictatorship Game

The dictatorship game is simply the ultimatum game in which Player 2 has no say: whatever Player 1 demands is implemented. Recall that in the ultimatum game, players may make generous offers not because they are altruistic, but because they fear that greedy offers will be rejected. The dictatorship game eliminates this possibility.

Without social preferences, the only equilibrium demand is the entire surplus, $x=1$. With social preferences, however, the reasoning above shows that Player 1 demands $x=\tfrac{1}{2}$ in equilibrium when $\beta\gt\tfrac{1}{2}$.

Alternative Equilibrium Concepts

The introduction of social preferences allows us to explain some of the experimental results, but it keeps the standard assumption that individuals play according to a Nash equilibrium or, in dynamic games, according to a subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium.

Two related alternative concepts that assume individuals use a more limited reasoning than implied by Nash equilibrium are:

  • Iterative deletion of dominated strategies.
  • Level $k$ reasoning.

Iterative Deletion of Dominated Actions (ID)

Definition: A player's strategy $s$ is strictly dominated if the player has an alternative strategy that yields a strictly higher payoff regardless of the other players' choices: $u_i(s,y)\lt u_i(t,y)$ for all $y$, where $y$ is the list of strategies chosen by the others.

A rational player would never play a strictly dominated action. So we may remove these actions from the game. However, the resulting game might have strictly dominated actions that were not strictly dominated in the original game. The process of eliminating strictly dominated actions and obtaining a simpler game will eventually stop (provided the game has finitely many strategies). The strategies that survive the iterative process of deletion of strictly dominated actions are ID's prediction.

In the first step, we only assume the players are rational. In the second, we assume they know that other players are rational. In the third, we assume that they know that the others know that they know… and so on.

Level $k$

  • Level zero behavior is given (pre-specified by the modeler).
  • Level one behavior is the best response to level zero behavior.
  • …
  • Level $k$ behavior is the best response to level $k-1$.
  • Level $+\infty$ is the limit of level $k$ as $k$ goes to infinity (and in some games it coincides with Nash equilibrium).
