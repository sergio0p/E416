Game Theory

A Quick Review

The strategy profile $a_{-i}$ is a list of actions of players distinct from player $i$. The best response of player $i$ is the set of all actions $x$ that maximize $u_i(x,a_{-i})$: the payoff of player $i$ when the others play according to $a_{-i}$. We write $BR_i(a_{-i})$ for the best response function of player $i$.

A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile $s=(s_1,\ldots,s_n)$ such that $s_i=BR_i(s_{-i})$ — every player is best responding to the others' actions.

Prisoners' Dilemma

The only equilibrium is for both players to shirk. Moreover, the best response is always to shirk regardless of the other player's strategy. However, in experiments, players often choose effort.

Nash Equilibrium

of Games with a Continuum of Actions

Consider a two-player game in which player 1 chooses $x$, player 2 chooses $y$, and the payoffs are $u_1(x,y)$ and $u_2(x,y)$. Assuming that marginal utilities of one's own action are decreasing, we obtain player 1's best response function by solving: $$ \diff{}{x}u_1(x,y)=0$$ and player 2's best response by solving $$\diff{ }{y}u_2(x,y)=0.$$ To obtain the Nash equilibria we solve the system of equations given by the best response functions: $$x=BR_1(y),\qquad y=BR_2(x).$$

Dynamic Games

In dynamic games, a strategy of a player prescribes the action that the player will play at each node in the game tree in which the player moves. We focus attention on subgame perfect Nash equilibrium, which is a Nash equilibrium in which at every node of the tree (including nodes that might not be reached during play) players are always best responding.

Continuation

Player 1 anticipates player 2's best response and chooses $x$ to maximize his/her payoff given player 2's best response: $$u_1(x,BR_2(x)),$$ so player 1's best response is found by solving $$\ddiff{}{x}u_1(x,BR_2(x))=\diff{}{x}u_1(x,BR_2(x))+\diff{}{y}u_1(x,BR_2(x))\cdot \ddiff{BR_2(x)}{x}=0.$$

Games Studied by Behavioral Game Theory

Public Good Contribution

A player has to divide one dollar between private consumption $1-x_i$ and donation to a public good $x_i$. Notice that for public goods, consumption is non-rival. The payoffs are: $$u_i(x)=1-x_i+\alpha\cdot \sum_{j=1}^n x_j,$$ where $1/n\lt \alpha\lt 1$ and $n$ is the number of players.

Notice that since $\diff{u_i}{x_i}=-1+\alpha\lt 0$, we have $BR_i(x_{-i})=0$ but $u_i(0,\ldots,0)=1\lt u_i(1,\ldots,1)=\alpha\cdot n$.

Player 1 makes demand $x$ and Player 2, after observing it, says either yes or no. Payoffs are: $u_1(x,y)=x$, $u_2(x,y)=1-x$, and $u_1(x,n)=u_2(x,n)=0.$

  • Player 2 is indifferent between saying yes or no to $x=1$.

Behavioral Game Theory Models

Social Preferences

Let $\mathbf{u}=(u_1,\ldots,u_n)$ be the standard payoffs of a game with $n$ players. Behavioral Game Theory assumes that players have social preferences, that is, their utility depends not only on their own payoffs but also on the payoffs of the other players. Thus, the utility of player $i$ is given by $U_i$ where:

  • Inequity aversion model ($0\lt \beta\lt \min(1,\alpha)$): $$U_i(\mathbf{u})=u_i-\beta\sum_{j\neq i} \underbrace{\max(u_i-u_j,0)}_{\text{guilt}}-\alpha\sum_{j\neq i} \underbrace{\max(u_j-u_i,0)}_{\text{envy}}.$$
  • ERC (equity-reciprocity-competition — not covered), with $\partial_1 F\gt 0$, $\partial_2 F(u_i,1/n)=0$ and $\partial^2_2 F(u_i,1/n)\lt 0$: $$U_i(\mathbf{u})=F\left(u_i, \dfrac{u_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} u_j}\right).$$

Alternative Equilibrium Concepts

The introduction of social preferences allows us to explain some of the experimental results, but it keeps the standard assumption that individuals play according to a Nash equilibrium or, in dynamic games, according to a subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium.

Two related alternative concepts that assume individuals use a more limited reasoning than implied by Nash equilibrium are:

  • Iterative deletion of dominated strategies.
  • Level $k$ reasoning.

Iterative Deletion of Dominated Actions (ID)

Definition: A player's strategy $s$ is strictly dominated if the player has an alternative strategy that yields a strictly higher payoff regardless of the other players' choices: $u_i(s,y)\lt u_i(t,y)$ for all $y$, where $y$ is the list of strategies chosen by the others.

A rational player would never play a strictly dominated action. So we may remove these actions from the game. However, the resulting game might have strictly dominated actions that were not strictly dominated in the original game. The process of eliminating strictly dominated actions and obtaining a simpler game will eventually stop (provided the game has finitely many strategies). The strategies that survive the iterative process of deletion of strictly dominated actions are ID's prediction.

In the first step, we only assume the players are rational. In the second, we assume they know that other players are rational. In the third, we assume that they know that the others know that they know… and so on.

Level $k$

  • Level zero behavior is given (pre-specified by the modeler).
  • Level one behavior is the best response to level zero behavior.
  • …
  • Level $k$ behavior is the best response to level $k-1$.
  • Level $+\infty$ is the limit of level $k$ as $k$ goes to infinity (and in some games it coincides with Nash equilibrium).
