Prospect Theory

Problem 1: The Value Function

Question

Choose the most appropriate answer for the value function of prospect theory.

  1. The argument $x$ of the value function $v(x)$ is the absolute level of income.
  2. The argument $x$ of the value function $v(x)$ is the difference from the reference point.
  3. Because of loss aversion, the slope of the value function $v(x)$ is larger when $x$ is negative than when $x$ is positive.
  4. Because of loss aversion, the slope of the value function $v(x)$ is smaller when $x$ is negative than when $x$ is positive.
  5. The typical value function implies risk aversion in the domain of losses and risk loving in the domain of gains.
  6. The typical value function implies risk aversion in the domain of gains and risk loving in the domain of losses.
  7. a, c, and e
  8. a, c, and f
  9. a, d, and e
  10. a, d, and f
  11. b, c, and e
  12. b, c, and f
  13. b, d, and e
  14. b, d, and f
Answer

(l): b, c, and f.

  • (b) Outcomes are gains and losses measured from the reference point.
  • (c) Loss aversion: $v$ is steeper for losses than for gains.
  • (f) Risk averse over gains, risk loving over losses.

Ogaki & Tanaka (2017), Ch. 4, Multiple-Choice Problem 1

Prospect Theory

Problem 2: Reference Points

A person’s value function is ${v(x)=\sqrt{x/2}}$ for gains ${(x\ge 0)}$ and ${v(x)=-2\sqrt{\lvert x\rvert}}$ for losses ${(x\lt 0).}$ She faces the choice between a sure $\$2$ and a 50–50 gamble that pays $\$4$ if she wins and $\$0$ if she loses. Weight outcomes by their probabilities.

Question 1
Show that she is loss averse, in the sense that she suffers more when she loses $\$4$ than she benefits when she receives $\$4$.
Answer 1
$$v(4)=\sqrt{2}\approx 1.41,\qquad v(-4)=-2\sqrt{4}=-4.$$ The loss is bigger in absolute value than the gain.
Question 2
If she takes the worst possible outcome ($\$0$) as her reference point, what is the value of the sure amount and of the gamble? Which does she prefer?
Answer 2
Coded as gains: $\$0\to 0$, $\$2\to +2$, $\$4\to +4$. $$v(2)=1,\qquad \tfrac12 v(0)+\tfrac12 v(4)=\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\approx 0.71.$$ She prefers the sure $\$2$: risk averse over gains.
Question 3
If she takes the best possible outcome ($\$4$) as her reference point, what is the value of the sure amount and of the gamble? Which does she prefer?
Answer 3
Coded as losses: $\$0\to -4$, $\$2\to -2$, $\$4\to 0$. $$v(-2)=-2\sqrt2\approx -2.83,\qquad \tfrac12 v(-4)+\tfrac12 v(0)=-2.$$ She prefers the gamble: risk loving over losses. Same outcomes, different reference point, opposite choice.

Angner (2016), Exercise 7.9

Prospect Theory

Problem 3: The Allais Paradox

Consider the following four lotteries, with prizes in billions of francs:

  • Lottery 1: $1$ for sure.
  • Lottery 2: $0$ with probability $0.01$, $5$ with probability $0.10$, and $1$ with probability $0.89$.
  • Lottery 3: $1$ with probability $0.11$, and $0$ with probability $0.89$.
  • Lottery 4: $5$ with probability $0.10$, and $0$ with probability $0.90$.

Asked to choose between Lotteries 1 and 2, many people choose Lottery 1. Asked to choose between Lotteries 3 and 4, many people choose Lottery 4. This pattern of choices is the Allais paradox.

Question 1
Show that no expected utility maximizer, whatever her utility function $u$, can make both choices.
Answer 1
Lottery 1 over Lottery 2 (cancel $0.89\,u(1)$): $$u(1)\gt 0.01\,u(0)+0.10\,u(5)+0.89\,u(1)\iff 0.11\,u(1)\gt 0.01\,u(0)+0.10\,u(5).$$ Lottery 4 over Lottery 3 (cancel $0.89\,u(0)$): $$0.10\,u(5)+0.90\,u(0)\gt 0.11\,u(1)+0.89\,u(0)\iff 0.11\,u(1)\lt 0.01\,u(0)+0.10\,u(5).$$ The two inequalities contradict each other, whatever $u$ is.
Question 2
Explain the Allais paradox using Prospect Theory decision weights $\pi(p)$.
Answer 2
Reference point: current wealth, so $v(0)=0$. Lottery 1 over Lottery 2: $$v(1)\gt\pi(0.10)\,v(5)+\pi(0.89)\,v(1)\iff\bigl(1-\pi(0.89)\bigr)\,v(1)\gt\pi(0.10)\,v(5).$$ Lottery 4 over Lottery 3: $$\pi(0.10)\,v(5)\gt\pi(0.11)\,v(1).$$ Both choices are consistent exactly when ${\pi(0.11)+\pi(0.89)\lt 1,}$ which decision weights allow: ${\pi(p)+\pi(1-p)\le 1.}$ The sure prize gets full weight; the $0.11$ and $0.89$ chances together get less.

Lotteries from Ogaki & Tanaka (2017), Sec. 3.5.1; questions adapted from Ch. 4, Short Answer/Essay Problem 1

Prospect Theory

Problem 4: Probability Weights

Question
Behavioral economists have found that lotteries are an effective way to encourage behavioral change. A person may be more likely to fill in a survey or take a pill if offered a lottery ticket with a $1/1000$ probability of winning $\$1000$ than if offered a cash payment of $\$1$. This might seem counterintuitive, given that people are often risk averse. Use decision weights to explain why lottery tickets can be so appealing.
Answer
Sure dollar: $\pi(1)\,v(1)=v(1)$. Ticket: $\pi(1/1000)\,v(1000)$.
  • Risk averse over gains: ${v(1000)\lt 1000\,v(1)}$, which works against the ticket.
  • Small probabilities are overweighted: ${\pi(1/1000)\gt 1/1000.}$
If $\pi(1/1000)$ is large enough, ${\pi(1/1000)\,v(1000)\gt v(1)}$ and the ticket wins.

Angner (2016), Exercise 7.33

Ambiguity

Problem 5: Ambiguity Aversion

Question 1
You can bet on a coin toss: if the coin comes up heads you win, and if it comes up tails you lose. You are ambiguity averse. Would you rather bet on a fair coin (equal probabilities of heads and tails) or on a loaded coin with unknown, unequal probabilities of heads and tails?
Answer 1
The fair coin. Its probabilities are known; the loaded coin’s are not. Same choice as the known urn in the Ellsberg problem.
Question 2
You are invited to bet on one of three tennis games. In game 1, two extraordinarily good players face each other. In game 2, two extraordinarily poor players face each other. In game 3, one very good and one very bad player face each other, but you do not know which is which, so as far as you are concerned each player wins with probability $50\%$. You are ambiguity averse. Which game would you be least likely to bet on? Why?
Answer 2
Game 3. In games 1 and 2 the players are evenly matched, so $50\%$ is a known probability. In game 3 the true probability is close to $0$ or to $1$, and you do not know which: $50\%$ only averages your ignorance. That is the most ambiguous bet.

Angner (2016), Exercises 7.26 and 7.27

Tversky & Kahneman (1981)

Problem 6: Framing of Outcomes

Question 1
Define the framing effect and give one example.
Answer 1

Framing effect: different descriptions of the same choice lead to different decisions.

Asian disease (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981), $600$ lives at stake:

  • Gain frame, “$200$ people will be saved” vs. a $1/3$ chance that $600$ are saved: $72\%$ choose the sure program (risk averse).
  • Loss frame, “$400$ people will die” vs. a $1/3$ chance that nobody dies: $78\%$ choose the gamble (risk loving).

Same programs; only the frame changed.

Question 2

Consider two problems:

  • In addition to whatever you own, you have been given $\$1000$. Choose between (A) a $50\%$ chance of winning $\$1000$ and (B) winning $\$500$ for sure.
  • In addition to whatever you own, you have been given $\$2000$. Choose between (C) a $50\%$ chance of losing $\$1000$ and (D) losing $\$500$ for sure.

In final outcomes, A is the same as C, and B is the same as D. Yet $84\%$ of participants chose B in the first problem and $69\%$ chose C in the second.

Suppose your value function is ${v(x)=\sqrt{x/2}}$ for gains and ${v(x)=-2\sqrt{\lvert x\rvert}}$ for losses, and that you have integrated the gift into your reference point. Weighting outcomes by their probabilities, find the values of A, B, C, and D. Which do you choose in each problem?

Answer 2
$$v(A)=\tfrac12\sqrt{500}\approx 11.18,\qquad v(B)=\sqrt{250}\approx 15.81\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{B}.$$ $$v(C)=\tfrac12\bigl(-2\sqrt{1000}\bigr)\approx -31.62,\qquad v(D)=-2\sqrt{500}\approx -44.72\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{C}.$$ The gift sets the reference point: the first problem is framed as gains (risk averse), the second as losses (risk loving).

Question 1: Ogaki & Tanaka (2017), Ch. 5, Short Answer/Essay Problem 2. Question 2: Angner (2016), Example 7.7 and Exercise 7.8

Mental Accounting

Problem 7: Hedonic Editing

Suppose your value function is ${v(x)=\sqrt{x/3}}$ for gains ${(x\ge 0)}$ and ${v(x)=-3\sqrt{\lvert x\rvert}}$ for losses ${(x\lt 0).}$

Question 1
Yesterday you had a decent day: you received a $\$48$ tax refund, and then an old friend repaid a $\$27$ loan you had forgotten about. What is the total value if you integrate the two gains? If you segregate them? From the point of view of value, is it better to integrate or to segregate?
Answer 1
$$\text{Integrate: } v(75)=\sqrt{25}=5.\qquad\text{Segregate: } v(48)+v(27)=4+3=7.$$ Segregate: risk averse over gains.
Question 2
Another day was terrible: you got a $\$144$ speeding ticket on your way to the opera, and then had to pay $\$25$ for a ticket you thought would be free. What is the total value if you integrate the two losses? If you segregate them? Which is better?
Answer 2
$$\text{Integrate: } v(-169)=-3\cdot 13=-39.\qquad\text{Segregate: } v(-144)+v(-25)=-36-15=-51.$$ Integrate: risk loving over losses.

Angner (2016), Exercises 7.12 and 7.16

Dynamic Consistency

Problem 8: Cancer Screening

Colonoscopies detect polyps before they become cancerous, but they are experienced as embarrassing and painful. The typical person, when young, resolves to have a colonoscopy when older, but changes his or her mind as the procedure approaches. Two patients, Abelita and Benny, choose between:

  • option $a$: a colonoscopy at ${t=1}$ (utility $0$) and being healthy at ${t=2}$ (utility $18$);
  • option $b$: no colonoscopy at ${t=1}$ (utility $6$) and being unhealthy at ${t=2}$ (utility $0$).

Abelita discounts the future exponentially with ${\delta=2/3.}$ Benny is a $(\beta,\delta)$-individual with ${\beta=1/6}$ and ${\delta=1.}$

Question 1
What are Abelita’s utilities of $a$ and $b$ at ${t=0}$? At ${t=1}$?
Answer 1
$$t=0:\quad U(a)=\left(\tfrac23\right)^2\cdot 18=8,\qquad U(b)=\tfrac23\cdot 6=4.$$ $$t=1:\quad U(a)=0+\tfrac23\cdot 18=12,\qquad U(b)=6.$$ She prefers $a$ at both dates: time consistent.
Question 2
What are Benny’s utilities of $a$ and $b$ at ${t=0}$? At ${t=1}$?
Answer 2
$$t=0:\quad U(a)=\tfrac16\,(0+18)=3,\qquad U(b)=\tfrac16\cdot 6=1.$$ $$t=1:\quad U(a)=0+\tfrac16\cdot 18=3,\qquad U(b)=6.$$ He prefers $a$ at ${t=0}$ but $b$ at ${t=1}$: a preference reversal.
Question 3
Who acts more like the typical patient? Who is more likely to end up with health issues?
Answer 3
Benny on both counts: he plans the colonoscopy, then skips it when the time comes.

Angner (2016), Exercise 9.5

Naive and Sophisticated Agents

Problem 9: Addiction

Life has three periods: youth, middle age, and old age. In every period you decide whether to do drugs (“hit”) or not (“refrain”). If you are not hooked, hitting gives utility $10$ and refraining $0$. If you are hooked, hitting gives $-8$ and refraining $-25$. In youth you are not hooked; after that, you are hooked exactly when you hit in the previous period. Let ${\delta=1}$ and ${\beta=1/2.}$ What do you do in each period if you are:

Question 1
time consistent (${\beta=1}$)?
Answer 1
  • Old age: no future, so always hit ($10\gt 0$; ${-8\gt -25}$).
  • Middle age, not hooked: hit ${10-8=2}$, refrain ${0+10=10}$: refrain. Hooked: hit ${-8-8=-16}$, refrain ${-25+10=-15}$: refrain.
  • Youth: hit ${10-15=-5}$, refrain ${0+10=10}$: refrain.
Refrain, refrain, hit.
Question 2
time inconsistent and naive?
Answer 2
  • Youth expects future selves to follow its own plan: refrain ${0+\tfrac12(0+10)=5}$, hit ${10+\tfrac12(-25+10)=2.5}$: refrain.
  • Middle age, not hooked: hit ${10+\tfrac12(-8)=6}$, refrain ${0+\tfrac12(10)=5}$: hit.
  • Old age, hooked: hit.
Refrain, hit, hit.
Question 3
time inconsistent and sophisticated?
Answer 3
  • Old age: hit.
  • Middle age, not hooked: hit (${6\gt 5}$). Hooked: hit ${-8+\tfrac12(-8)=-12}$, refrain ${-25+\tfrac12(10)=-20}$: hit.
  • Youth knows the middle-aged self will hit either way: hit ${10+\tfrac12(-8-8)=2}$, refrain ${0+\tfrac12(10-8)=1}$: hit.
Hit, hit, hit.

Angner (2016), Exercise 9.22

Preferences Over Utility Streams

Problem 10: Saving the Best for Last

Question 1

It is Sunday morning ${(t=0)}$, and you will clean the apartment and go to the movies today: either clean in the morning and go to the movies in the afternoon ${(t=1)}$, or go to the movies in the morning and clean in the afternoon. Cleaning gives you utility $2$; the movies give you $12$. From the point of view of ${t=0}$, what is the utility of cleaning first and of going to the movies first if:

  • you discount exponentially with ${\delta=1/2}$?
  • you are a $(\beta,\delta)$-individual with ${\beta=1/3}$ and ${\delta=1/2}$?
Answer 1
$$\text{Exponential: clean first } 2+\tfrac12\cdot 12=8,\qquad\text{movies first } 12+\tfrac12\cdot 2=13.$$ $$(\beta,\delta)\text{: clean first } 2+\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac12\cdot 12=4,\qquad\text{movies first } 12+\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac12\cdot 2\approx 12.33.$$ Both models schedule the pleasant experience first, yet people often clean first and keep the movies for last.
Question 2
Suppose you add a pleasant tail to an already pleasant episode. If people evaluate the episode with the peak-end rule, will this make them think of the episode as a whole as more or less pleasant?
Answer 2
Less pleasant, when the tail is milder than the episode’s end: the peak is unchanged and the end utility falls, so ${U(\mathbf{u})=f\bigl(\max_t u_t,\,u_T\bigr)}$ falls. The extra pleasant time does not count (duration neglect).

Angner (2016), Exercises 9.12 and 9.14
