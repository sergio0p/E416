Consumer Choice

The Standard Model

Consider a set $X$ of alternatives. Think primarily of $X$ as the set of possible baskets of consumption goods/services, $x=(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n)$ in $X$ is a basket that contains $x_1$ units of good 1, $x_2$ units of good 2, ..., $x_n$ units of good $n$.

The standard model assumes that any individual is able to compare and rank the elements of $X$. Fix some individual $i$.

Individual Preference Relations

We write $\mathbf{x} \succeq_i \mathbf{y}$ to say that individual $i$ considers the basket $\mathbf{x}$ at least as good as basket $\mathbf{y}$.

And $\mathbf{x} \succ_i \mathbf{y}$ means that individual $i$ considers the basket $\mathbf{x}$ strictly better than the basket $\mathbf{y}$.

We call the ranking $\succ_i$ individual $i$'s preferences.

Preferences

The Standard Assumptions

Given a choice set $X$ and individual $i$ with preferences $\succeq$ we shall assume:

  • Ability to rank (aka Completeness): for any two baskets $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ then $\mathbf{a}\succeq \mathbf{b}$ or $\mathbf{b}\succeq \mathbf{a}$.
  • Coherence (aka Transitivity): for any three baskets $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{c}$: $\mathbf{a}\succeq \mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{b}\succeq \mathbf{c}$ together imply that $\mathbf{a}\succeq \mathbf{c}$.
  • More is better (I) (aka Monotonicity): for any two baskets, $\mathbf{a}=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$ and $\mathbf{b}=(b_1,\ldots,b_n)$, whenever $a_1\ge b_1,\ldots,a_n\ge b_n$ it must be that $\mathbf{a}\succeq \mathbf{b}$.
  • More is better (II) (aka Strict Monotonicity): for any two baskets, $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, whenever $a_1\ge b_1,\ldots,a_n\ge b_n$ and $\mathbf{a}\neq \mathbf{b}$, it must be that $\mathbf{a}\succ \mathbf{b}$.
  • Continuity: for any basket $\mathbf{a}$ and a sequence of baskets $\mathbf{b}_n$: if $\mathbf{a}\succ \mathbf{b}_n$ and $\mathbf{b}=\lim b_n$ then $\mathbf{a}\succeq \mathbf{b}$.

Assumptions on Preferences

Remarks

  1. When $\mathbf{a}\succeq \mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{b}\succeq \mathbf{a}$, we say that the individual is indifferent between the baskets $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ and we write $\mathbf{a}\sim \mathbf{b}$.
  2. It is (implicitly) assumed that an individual's preferences are stable: they remain the same (at least for the period of our analysis).
  3. Also, it is (implicitly) assumed that preferences are invariant with respect to different representations. That is, if $(x,y)$ and $(x,y)$ are different representations of the same basket, then $(x,y)$ $\sim$ $(x,y)$.
  4. But the standard model does not assume one is conscious of one's own preferences. If an individual chooses basket $\mathbf{a}$ over basket $\mathbf{b}$, we will say that he/she reveals to prefer the basket $\mathbf{a}$ over the basket $\mathbf{b}$, $\mathbf{a}\succeq \mathbf{b}$.

Limitations of the Standard Model Assumptions

  1. Completeness rules out incomplete preferences.
    (being unable to judge is not possible).
  2. Invariance rules out framing effects.
  3. Stability rules out reference dependent preferences.

Utility Functions

  • An utility function $u_i$ is a scale for some preference $\succeq_i$. An utility function is just a convenient way of converting the rankings given by the preference to some numerical scale.
  • We say that the utility function $u_i$ represents the preference $\succeq_i$ if for any two baskets $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$: $$\mathbf{a}\succeq_i \mathbf{b}\Leftrightarrow u_i(\mathbf{a})\ge u_i(\mathbf{b}).$$
  • As there are many temperature scales (Fahrenheit, Celcius, Kelvin, etc.), there are also infinitely many possible different scales (utility functions) that represent the same preference.

Representation Theorems

  1. If the set of all available baskets is finite and preferences satisfy: ability to rank and coherence then there is a utility representation.
  2. If the set of all available baskets is convex (i.e., any basket on the line connecting two available baskets is also available) and preferences satisfy: ability to rank, coherence, more is better and continuity then there is an utility representation.

Extracting (an) Utility From the Preferences

A Recipe

  1. For any basket in the diagonal $(x,x)$ its utility is equal to $u(x,x)=x$.
  2. For any other basket $c$, find a basket in the diagonal $d$ such that $c\sim d$ and set $u(c)=u(d)$.

What means for an economic agent to be rational?

We say that an economic agent is rational if her choices can be explained (are compatible) with some preference that satisfy: ability to rank, coherence, more is better, and continuity.

Revealed Preferences

Data

We want to test whether a consumer's choices are rational. So we consider a sequence of prices and choices $(\mathbf{p}^t, \mathbf{x}^t)$, $t=1,\ldots,T$ that the consumer made over time.

Notice that $\mathbf{p}^t$ is a list of prices prevailing at time $t$. For example, in the case of two goods, X and Y: $\mathbf{p}^t=(p^t_{x},p^t_{y})$ and $\mathbf{x}^t=(x^{t},y^{t})$ is the basket chosen by the consumer at period $t$.

To save notation, we write $\mathbf{p}^t\cdot \mathbf{x}^t$ for the value of the basket under the prices, so $\mathbf{p}^t\cdot \mathbf{x}^t=p^t_{x}x^{t}+p^t_{y}y^{t}$.

Revealed Preferences

Definitions

Consider a sequence of prices and choices $(\mathbf{p}^t, \mathbf{x}^t)$, $t=1,\ldots,T$.

Definition (Direct Revelation)
If $\mathbf{p}^t \cdot \mathbf{x}^s \le \mathbf{p}^t \cdot \mathbf{x}^t$ then $\mathbf{x}^t$ is directly revealed to be preferred to $\mathbf{x}^s$. We write this as $\mathbf{x}^t \succeq_{DR} \mathbf{x}^s$.
Definition (Strict Direct Revelation)
If $\mathbf{p}^t \cdot \mathbf{x}^s \lt \mathbf{p}^t \cdot \mathbf{x}^t$ then $\mathbf{x}^t$ is strictly directly revealed to be preferred to $\mathbf{x}^s$. We write this as $\mathbf{x}^t \succ_{SDR} \mathbf{x}^s$.
Definition (Indirect Revelation)
If $\mathbf{x}_{k} \succeq_{DR} \mathbf{x}_{k+1}$ for $k=0,1,2,\ldots,K-1$, we say that $\mathbf{x}_0$ is indirectly revealed to be preferred to $\mathbf{x}_K$. We write this as $\mathbf{x}_0\succeq_{IR} \mathbf{x}_K$.

Revealed Preferences

GARP

Consider a sequence of prices and choices $(\mathbf{p}^t, \mathbf{x}^t)$, $t=1,\ldots,T$.

If $\mathbf{x} \succeq_{DR} \mathbf{y}$ or $\mathbf{x} \succeq_{IR} \mathbf{y}$ we say that $\mathbf{x}$ is revealed to be preferred to $\mathbf{y}$ and write $\mathbf{x} \succeq_R \mathbf{y}$.

GARP (Generalized Axiom of Revealed Preference)
If $\mathbf{x} \succeq_R \mathbf{y}$ then it is NOT the case that $\mathbf{y} \succ_{SDR} \mathbf{x}$.

Main results:

  1. If GARP is satisfied for all the choices in the data then there is a preference that satisfies all the standard assumptions (ability to rank, coherence, more is better and continuity) and that also generates these choices.
  2. If GARP is violated, there are no rational preferences that can rationalize the data.

Behavioral Rules

Assume two goods (X and Y) for simplicity and imagine that instead of maximizing some utility, the consumer chooses her optimal basket according to one of the behavioral rules below — we are assuming our consumer follows just one of the rules. We cannot dismiss the consumer as being irrational because perhaps the behavioral rule can be generated by rational preferences.

  1. Spend 30% of income on good X and 70% of income on good Y.
  2. Spend 20% of income on the cheapest good and 80% on the more expensive good.
  3. Spend 90% of income on the cheapest good and 10% on the more expensive good.
